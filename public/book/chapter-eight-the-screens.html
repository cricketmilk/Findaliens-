Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Eight — The Screens

The summons came the way real summonses came, and after the quartermaster's chit he found he had lost the knack of taking the liturgy seriously, which was dangerous, and he noted the danger, a reagent past its date. Grey paper. The Song's cipher in white wax. A robed clerk who entered his floor at the second hour, stood while Aurelius rose, and waited while he straightened his coat, because the waiting was part of the instrument. You were not sent for by the halls. You were collected.

Four flights up, past the refectories, the clerk's sandals keeping the pace that such sandals kept, and Aurelius climbed inside his own attention like a man checking a kit before weather. The blanket case stayed below. The wire stayed in the pocket. The pocket also held, because removing them had seemed more legible than keeping them, a folded chit and a plait of waxed cord, and he was aware of them against his side the whole climb.

He had stood before the screens four times in his service. Program charters, appropriations, once for the border commendations. He knew the room the way he knew all rooms, entirely, and it had been built by someone who understood that knowing a room entirely could be made into a burden: a long oval chamber with no windows, lit from a ring of lamps above, and around its far curve the seven screens, black lacquer frames and grey silk panels, lamplight behind them arranged so that what reached the silk was glow without figure. No shadows sat on the screens. That was the room's one lesson, taught to every visitor's animal brain in the first three seconds: you faced seven lit panels of nothing, and the nothing faced back.

They said the screens' silk was woven with silverthread, and that it took the wrist out of a voice the way the dormitory schools took it out of a hand. He had believed it for years on the evidence of his ears. Every voice that came through the screens arrived level, mid deep, paceless, the same voice seven times, a voice with no province. The Chorus had standardized the hand, and then the face, and then the voice. Somewhere at the end of that series, he had thought once, at his first appearance, young and impressed, lay a perfected institution that was no one at all, speaking to itself in a sealed room, forever.

In the chamber's center, on a plain iron stand at the focus of the oval, sat a recording stone the size of two fists, dark, housed in brass, its cradle threaded into the floor.

And at a table before the screens, in the position a defending advocate would occupy if the Concord's law had ever imagined anyone defending anything here, sat Kestrel Voss.

She did not rise. She was a short, compact woman in her sixties who sat the way freight sits, settled, total, and she wore grey wool where every other soul on the hall floors wore robes, and before her on the table lay a black ledger, closed, and his floor's winter reconciliation, open, with his own certification at its foot, nine days old.

He had spoken with Voss twice in all his years at the Needle. He could have recited both conversations. The first, a dozen years ago, at the program's second appropriations, had run to twenty two words on her side, of which nine had been numbers. The second, four years ago, in a corridor, had run to seven: Your birds cost less than they save. He had thought about that sentence for a season before deciding it was the finest compliment the Empire had ever paid him, and the coldest, and that in her accounting there was no difference.

"Magister. Sit."

Two.

He sat. The chair faced the screens, but the screens did not speak. This session belonged to the woman at the table, and the seven lit panels of nothing attended it, and the stone in the middle of the room did what stones did.

She did not consult notes. She looked at him with eyes that had already inventoried the room, him included, his scarred hands included, filed by weight and condition, and she asked her questions in the voice she asked everything, the voice of a woman reading a manifest back to a dockhand who might yet be honest.

"Your floor alone performs the seventh distillation."

"Yes."

"Your registers are complete."

"Yes."

A pause of no length; she did not pause for effect; she paused for order. Then:

"In the past quarter, has any person, cleared or otherwise, raised the preparation with you outside of session or requisition?"

He heard the door in it.

He heard it the way he heard drafts in corners, instantly, bodily, before thought. Outside of session or requisition. The woman with the folder had come inside requisition, wearing requisition, carrying requisition's dullest paper. The question as drafted walked him past her visit the way a rail walks a man past a drop, and he had answered truthfully before his pulse had finished the first syllable.

"No."

Someone had built that question with an exit in it. Voss did not build exits; Voss built manifests; a fact was in her inventory or it was not, and phrasing was a place where facts got lost, and she would no sooner have drafted that clause than she would have misweighed cargo out of kindness. The questions had been prepared for her. Somewhere upstream of this table, in the secretariat that dressed the council's business, a hand had written him a door into the official record, in a clause of standard boilerplate no auditor would ever read twice.

He was being examined and protected in the same breath, by instruments he could not see, in a room where he could not look at the one thing he wanted to look at, which was the seven panels of glowing silk, one of which, or none of which, held a person who reconciled.

"Are you aware of any irregularity in the circulation of council papers."

No door in that one. It stood plain, load bearing, the question the whole liturgy had been assembled to carry, and the room came to its point around it: the stone drinking, the screens attending, Voss's eyes at rest on his face with the patience of scales.

"No," Aurelius said.

One word, level, exactly cut. Nothing in his body attended it; the animal that heard drafts in corners heard nothing go by at all. And he heard it come out of him in the session voice, the voice for the chair and the chalice, very short and very precise, and heard, in the same instant, what it was. His tell. The sound his speech made when he was hiding something, named long ago by his own private audit and never once corrected, because there had never been anyone across the table who both knew him and mattered. He listened to the word land in the silk and the stone and the wool, and he could not tell, facing seven panels of arranged glow, whether anywhere behind them sat the whole history of the table, listening to a man go precise.

Voss regarded him for a moment longer. If the word had weighed wrong on her scales, the scales did not say. She turned the reconciliation to face him, one finger on the summary band, on the aggregate he had read nine days ago in front of a woman made of features that occurred elsewhere.

"This number will grow," she said. "Your floor's share of it will not. Adjust nothing. Certify what is yours."

"Understood."

She closed the reconciliation, and squared it against the ledger's edge, and for the first and only time in the session something in her voice shifted, not softening, nothing in Voss softened, but settling, going from manifest to bill of lading, the freight inspected and accepted and the account ruled off.

"Irregularities of paper resolve as paper," Voss said. "While they can. Good day, Magister."

Eleven words to close, forty one in all. He counted them on the stairs without meaning to. Two conversations in fourteen years, and now a third, and each one shorter than the last, and he understood, descending, that in her ledger this was not curtness. It was refinement. She spent words on a man while his account stood open. Hers with him was closing toward a figure, entry by entry, and the figure would be reached in some season not of his choosing, and it would balance, and that would be that.

He did not go to his floor. He went up, to the aerie, into the wind, because the tower's air had acquired a pressure the instruments would not have found, and Cinder took one look at him along her amber eye and kept the second of her silences, the danger one, and he stood at the parapet with the cold working through his coat and conducted, because he was what he was, the audit.

What they knew: that the papers had moved. The summons proved that much; you did not convene silk and stone for a hypothesis. What they did not know: where. If any soul behind the screens had known the packet stood behind his blood lock, this morning would not have been questions. It would have been resolved as people.

What he had done: lied. On the record, into the stone, before the seven. The word was down in crystal now, in a drawer somewhere below with his name on the card, harvested like everything else, and there was no line of retreat from it, no version of any future in which that no could be reread as innocence. Until this morning he had been a man who had received things: a chit, a packet, a folder, a door built into a question. Received was survivable. Received could be reported late, explained, forgiven by a mentor with real warmth over real wine. Spoken could not. One syllable, and he had stopped being a man who had not yet decided, and the deciding had felt like nothing at all, that was the finding that kept the wire moving in his pocket, it had felt like procedure, like the next line in a form he had been filling out his whole life without reading the heading.

And someone had helped him do it. That was the residue at the bottom of the audit, the part that would not evaporate. The door in the question. The exit, drafted in advance, in boilerplate, by a hand with no wrist, threaded through the council's own instrument by someone who had known three things: that the woman with the folder would visit, that the council would ask, and that he, shown a door, would walk through it and become, in the record's eyes, clean, and in his own, committed. He had been walked to the far side of a line with such care that his feet had never once felt the crossing.

Recruitment, then. He stood in the wind and retired the other two readings, not because the facts had changed but because the composition had declared itself: tests conclude, warnings end, and this had continued, and continuation was the signature. Somewhere below him, behind silk or wool or an administrator's middling grey, a craftsman was assembling him, delivery by delivery, into something, with a patience he recognized because he had eaten beside it at a monthly table and flown its results over the eastern grass.

The wind came over the parapet with the smell of snow in it. Cinder shifted on the perch, wing to wing, and fixed him with the eye.

"I know," he said. "I signed."

Far below, in the cold floors, a stone the size of two fists was carried by a clerk down a corridor, on a tray, toward the drawers, holding forty one words of Kestrel Voss and two of his, and of everything the Needle had ever taken from him and filed, he begrudged it those two the most. They were the truest thing he had said in this building, and the record would keep them as a lie, and the lie would keep him as a man, and there was no arithmetic anywhere in the tower that could rule those three facts into one column.

He went down to his floor. The boy was at the stands, cleaning glass that was already clean, which was what the boy did now when the tower's weather changed and no one told him why. Aurelius stood in the doorway a moment, watching those steady hands move, and entered, under the morning's date, the shortest finding of the winter:

Two of them now.
