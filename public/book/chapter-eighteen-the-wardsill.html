Part Two — The Breaking
Chapter Eighteen — The Wardsill

The counterwork began in the undermews in the first true heat of summer, in a distillation hall no survey had ever mapped, behind a lock that knew one man's blood, and it began with a failure that lasted twenty days.

He had framed the problem the way his training framed problems. The Rending would ride the archive's kinship and the collars' web; therefore, jam the resonance. He built dampers. He built baffles of silverthread wound against its own lay, chokes, mutes, a whole month of elegant interference, and every one of them worked, and every one of them was useless, and he knew it each time within the hour. A jammed stone read as a jammed stone. Interference was loud; it was a fist held up in front of the instrument's eye; and the halls' auditors would find the first muted drawer inside a season and follow it home in a week. Worse, the mutes deafened. A stone wrapped in his best baffle stopped receiving the Rending's flood and also stopped its own slow eleven, went wild, went alone, and he sat in the deep hall with his twentieth elegant failure and understood that he was solving the wrong problem with the whole weight of his life's skill, which was precisely the weight in his way.

The baffles mocked him from the bench while he failed, and not only for their uselessness. Silverthread, all of them, drawn from the same kilns as the enemy's arrays, because there were no other kilns: the Verdance grew the thread for every instrument on every side of every question, asked no side's name, only how much and how soon, and the counterwork itself ran on the adversary's own substance, bought at the adversary's own price, from a works that would sell the rope and the rescue with the same clerk's smile. There was no purity to be had anywhere in the age. There was only what a man chose to build with the thread.

He could not make the vessels unreachable. Unreachable was just another emptiness. What the vessels needed was what the anneal had taken from them first, before it took anything else, and he did not have a word for it yet, and the woman in the west did.

The taper circuit ran all that summer, and the sessions had changed their nature: less than half medicine now, the rest instruction. Thessine's restoration had reached the long strata, the deep rote of a keeper's training, and it was coming back not as memory but as curriculum, and she taught the way she kept fires, banked low, fed on schedule, without apology, at her own table, to a class of one.

"You are wrapping the stones," she said, when he described the baffles. It was not a question. She had taken to diagnosing his week from his hands before he spoke; he had stopped finding this remarkable. "Wrapping is burial. We do not bury the living. Tell me what the fens put over a door."

"Iron."

"And what does iron do?" She watched him reach for resonance theory and cut him off with a small motion, the schoolmistress motion, before the first word. "Not what your monographs say. What does it do? A house with iron over the door still has guests. Still has weather in its chimney, news at its window, milk going out its sill. Iron does not seal a house, Magister. If it sealed the house the house would die. Iron asks the things that come to the door to knock."

He sat with that for a moment, and twenty days of elegant failure quietly reorganized themselves into one plain finding: he had been building walls, and the folk had never once built a wall. They had built thresholds.

"Say the rest," he said. "The milk."

"You set it on the sill, not in the yard, not in the house. On the sill. The line itself." Her stained hands moved as she talked now, the old teaching gestures coming back up with the knowledge, shaping a doorway in the air above her table. "You do not feed what you fear inside your walls, and you do not starve it either, because starved things test doors. You feed it at the line, and the feeding says: there is a line. Guest and weather are different words. Everything the old way does, the iron, the milk, the fire order, the names, all of it says the one sentence, over and over, in every grammar we had." She looked at him. "There is a line. It can be kept. Nothing crosses but what is answered."

"And a soul," Aurelius said slowly, "that the array has emptied..."

"Has no sill." She said it the way she said the exact things, once, evenly, and let the weight of it stand in the warm room. "That is what you took, Magister, before you took anything else. Not the fires and not the names. The doorstep they stood on. A person with a sill can hear any story in the world knock and let it stand in the weather until it says its true name. A person without one," and her eyes went, deliberate, terrible, mild, to the window where her candle burned, "makes tea for whatever walks in. I know. I kept house that way for a season and a half."

He rode east the next morning with the whole counterwork inverted in his head, and the finding formulated itself somewhere on the fell road, in her cadence, because his own cadence had proved too proud for it:

Do not jam. Do not wrap. Do not wall. Build sills.

The technical work of it nearly defeated him.

The wardsill could not be pure. That was the discovery of the first week back in the deep hall. His seventh distillation, the perfected heart of the anneal, worked by totality: it softened everything evenly, every edge at once, and evenness was exactly wrong for a threshold, because a threshold is a discrimination, a place where some things pass and some things wait, and discrimination requires grain. Roughness. Difference held in the material itself. The preparation that could give a vessel a doorstep had to be distilled coarse, stopped at the third pass, left cloudy with its own particulars, and Aurelius, who had spent half a lifetime refining his hands to the finest tolerances in the Empire, spent that week learning, deliberately, to distill badly.

It was the hardest technical work of his life. His hands fought him at every stage; excellence was in them like the wire was in them, below decision; he ruined four batches by involuntary competence, correcting toward purity in gestures too small to catch, and on the fifth he finally managed it, managed crudeness, managed to leave a thing unfinished on purpose, and stood over the cloudy result understanding that the Mother had been trying to teach him this from the beginning. Tin and green glass. Mended at the throat. The whole art of giving back, waiting all these years in the shape of an instrument too rough to steal from. The Chorus's alchemy was a century's climb toward perfect evenness, and perfect evenness was the hollowing, and the cure was grain, was particularity, was the stubborn cloudy local unrepeatable stuff his whole discipline existed to distill away. He wrote the finding in the counterwork's book, the real book, the one that lived behind blood, and it was the shortest entry in it: Purity empties. Grain keeps.

The authentication he took from the fire order, and for that he needed nothing new at all; the four hundred and six had been carrying the protocol down in the dark all year. Eleven, hill to hill. The Rending's flood, when it came, would come as the wildest fire ever set, total, even, uncounted, everywhere at once; and a vessel with a wardsill, keyed to the count, would hear the difference the way any moor child could hear it, between the beacon line walking the tops at its patient elevens and a hillside simply alight. The count was the knock. The flood could not knock. It did not know there were doors.

The delivery was the mother batch's own gift, and he had Thessine's sentence for that too, the one from the night of her waking, which he had entered in the true book as a specification. The fire never went out; they had only put it in boxes; and the boxes were kin, all four hundred and six, wired together by his array through every drawer and every province, one family at eleven seconds, with her stone leading. The Chorus had built a single wire to every emptied soul in the Concord. It had never once occurred to anyone in the halls that wires carry both ways. He would not need to touch four hundred drawers. He would need to teach one stone the wardsill, the lead stone, the strongest, the teacher's own, and let the kinship do what kinship does, hill to hill, at the pace of the lighting, until every banked fire in the archive stood behind its own doorstep, waiting, tended, keeping count.

The first full trial ran on the last night of summer, in the deep hall, with the doors sealed and the lamps low.

On the bench: a drawn stone, one of the forty warranted for reseating, an emptied man of the Sixth Province, three years in the drawers, singing his eleven. Beside it, the projector rig Aurelius had built to play the enemy: a scaled fill, a little Rending, a manufactured narrative of comfort tuned from the stone's own impressions, exactly as the annexes specified, the vessel's own harvested life turned to a key for its own lock. He had run this rig against unprotected stones twice, to calibrate, and both times the fill had gone in like water into a cellar, total, silent, obscene, and he had reversed it after with the Mother and sat a long while before working again.

Tonight the stone wore the wardsill: three cloudy drops on the housing at the threshold points, iron filings in wax at the drawer's lip, the count keyed in. He engaged the rig. The coupling took him at the wrists, the old road inward, and the deep hall's air dropped and thickened, wax and iron and river cold, and behind his sternum a seam he had stopped reporting years ago opened by its familiar fraction. Copper on the tongue. He swallowed and read the instruments.

The fill rose, found the stone, and arrived at it. And stood.

The instruments drew it for him in lines he would keep behind his eyes for whatever remained of his life: the manufactured story pooling at the boundary like floodwater at a doorstep, pressing, insinuating, fluent, enormous, and the little vessel's resonance holding its own grain against the flood, unjammed, undeafened, still singing its eleven underneath, hill to hill, tended, and the fill could not cross, because crossing now required an answer, and the flood had no name to give. It was even, and evenness cannot knock. For eleven minutes the whole force of his life's perfected art stood in the deep hall outside one rough threshold and was, politely, in the oldest grammar in the world, not let in.

He shut the rig down. The stone sang on, untouched, keeping time.

Aurelius sat alone in the undermews with his twentieth failure's successor humming on the bench, and did not exult, exultation not being among his instruments, but he permitted himself, at length, the finding, entered in the true book under the summer's last date, in a hand that was almost steady:

The array emptied. The Mother filled. And the third thing did neither. It gave a soul back its doorstep and let it keep its own house. It was made of iron and milk and cloudy thirds and a count of eleven, none of it invented, all of it transcribed, from a curriculum the Empire had spent a century calling superstition and one keeper had carried through the dark in her hands.

Thessine had named it before he could, at her table, and the name was already in the book, and it was correct in every register he knew:

The wardsill. So that what is scattered, when the flood comes, may stand at its own door. And answer.
