Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Eleven — The Mother

Below the lowest archive floor there was a last brass door, and below the last brass door there was no book.

He had always found that eloquent. Above, the tower counted everything: hours, drawers, drams, the words of its own councils. The counting ran down through the cold floors like the silverthread ran down through the stone, and then, at a certain landing, at a certain door nobody serviced, it simply stopped, and the stairs went on without it. The Chorus had built downward in its first decades, hungry for storage, and had met the Ossuary, and had looked at what the mountain already held, and had, for the only time in its history that Aurelius knew of, declined to make a record. There was no inventory of the bone galleries. There was no survey. The most documented institution in the history of the world kept, at its root, a floor of deliberate ignorance, and if anyone had asked, the halls would have said the deep levels were unsuitable for storage, damp, unstable, and the halls would have been lying, because the deep levels were dry as old char, and stable as the mountain, and the truth was the one nobody wrote down: even engineers of belief had stood in that dark, and felt what was in it, and gone back up the stairs.

He went down them with a shaded lamp and an empty case, in the hour before the morning shift, and the cold changed around him as he went. The archive's cold was made: chased air, alchemy, a temperature with a maintenance schedule. This cold was old. It came up out of the rock the way meaning used to come up out of things, without machinery, and it smelled of dry stone and time, and at the ninth turning the last of the tower's hum fell away above him, and he stopped, as he always stopped, to listen to what was left.

The held breath of the drawers was gone. In its place, lower down, where hearing gives way to something older that listens through the bones of the skull, there was the other sound. He had no name for it and had never sought one. A hum, if a hum could be felt and not heard. Vast, patient, further down, in the galleries no lamp of his had ever reached, and it did not change, and it did not answer, and it was not the archive and it was not the wind in the mountain, and Cinder, who feared nothing that flew or walked, would not come below the aerie's floor, and had never once, in all her years, been willing to be carried past this landing.

He did not go toward it. He never went toward it. He turned, as always, along the first gallery, where the bones were.

A people no record named had laid their dead in the mountain long before the Empire, longer before the Chorus, and they had laid them with care, in niches cut clean into the living rock, rank over rank into the dark. Nobody knew what they had called themselves. The moor folk, if anyone had asked them, could have said at least what the mountain had been to such people, because the moor folk still climbed their own high places with fire and could recognize a threshold when they stood in one, but nobody had asked them, and so the Needle had been driven into the greatest high place in the world by men who catalogued everything and understood nothing, and the bones kept their counsel in the dark under the ledgers.

His lamp moved along the niches as he walked, and the dead attended it as they always did, incuriously, at rest in a way the drawers upstairs would never be. He had worked out, over the years, some of their grammar. The skulls faced inward, toward the mountain's heart, all of them, rank on rank, as if the rock itself were the thing worth watching. Shells from some sea that was nowhere near, one to a niche. And in the fourth gallery, in a niche he had found in his second year and visited ever since the way other men visited graves, there was the box.

Iron, rusted to lace, the size of two fists. It sat by the skull of someone who had been small, a woman or a child, and its lid had rusted open, and inside it, still, after whatever thousands of years, lay ash, and under the ash, charcoal, and the charcoal bore the marks of having been banked with skill.

A glede box. The uplands still made them; he had seen one on a crofter's hearth not a month ago, riding to Harrow Fell. Some hand older than every name in every register upstairs had banked a coal with care, and closed the box, and set it beside their dead in the mountain, for the crossing, for the dark, for whatever the carrying was for, and the Chorus had built forty floors of the future on top of that gesture without ever once bending down to look at it.

He stood at the niche for his count of four. He took nothing. He never took anything. But this morning, for the first time, he stood there as a colleague.

His cache lay past the sixth gallery, behind a slab he had chosen because it looked like every other slab, in a chamber the size of a cell, and the lock he had grown across its seam was the elder sibling of the one on his workroom door, cruder and stronger, the way everything in this room was cruder and stronger. It knew his blood and let him in.

The private stone sat on its shelf in its wrappings, dark and patient. He had come down to it a few nights a year, year upon year, his other liturgy, the replays, the sittings, the visits nobody counted because nobody counted here, and he touched the wrappings once in greeting and let it be, because the stone was not what the empty case was for.

The Mother stood under sacking at the back, on the bench he had built for it out of scavenged rack wood, and he drew the sacking off and looked at his beginning.

It was an ugly instrument. He had never pretended otherwise, and Marrow had said so outright, the first week: a squat alembic of tin and green glass, blown by a border glazier who owed her for a difficult birth, mended twice at the throat with lead and once, later, with silver when there was silver, its cradle sized for one stone and one taper, its whole conception the length of her back room. No silverthread anywhere in it. No projector geometry. It predated all of that; it was the thing all of that had been refined out of, and the refining had been his life's proud work, and he stood looking at the original in the lamplight with the knowledge he had carried down the stairs like a second lamp: that everything the refining had added was the weapon, and everything it had discarded was the cure.

The Mother did not empty. That was its crudeness and its whole genius, and neither he nor Marrow had understood, then, that it was either. Built to let a softened self and its own recorded life sit together in one resonance, slowly, at the pace of a taper, with a human voice in the room doing the work no instrument did: it gave weight back. It re paid. The widow Casse had planted her garden out of this green glass throat. The art's law ran forward as well as back, and this was the forward, the only forward ever built, standing under sacking in a mountain's root because its own maker had been walked, gently, admiringly, year by year, into building nothing but reverse.

He made himself say the sum once, plainly, in the cell, where no instrument would carry it.

The drawers upstairs were an ember bed, four hundred kin and Thessine leading, and the papers behind his other lock were a schedule for pouring a manufactured heaven into every emptied vessel in the Concord on a night at the far midwinter, and between the embers and the schedule stood exactly one man, who had built the emptying, and one machine, which had only ever known how to fill, and the man had come down at last, with an empty case, to fetch the machine.

There was no triumph anywhere in him. He searched, honestly, the way he searched every room he entered, and found none. What stood in its place was dread, itemized: the reversal was untested on the hollowed; the Mother was built for a taper's pace and he had four hundred siblings and a clock; the first subject would have to be her, because her stone led and because the debt ran that way, and to reverse her was to hand back everything the array had taken, the fells, the fires, the weight, the grief, the memory of a cold chamber and a man's level voice reading charges; she would wake owning all of it, including him. And past her, in ranks, like the niches: the boy, the valley, Sarat on the winter grass, forty austringers and nine intakes and a bird on a high wall, every one of them a hostage the moment his hand moved, and his hand was moving. It had been moving, he understood, since a woman corrected a star's name across a ledger, and everything since had only been the rest of him catching up.

He packed the Mother into the case with the care he had once packed field kits for the border, throat padded, cradle wedged, and the case took it the way cases take things, indifferently, and became simply a case, carried by a senior of the fourth floor, who had every right to be on the stairs.

At the door of the cell he paused, and went back, and took the private stone as well.

He climbed, the case pulling his shoulder down at each turning. Past the ninth turning and its patient hum, past the bone galleries with their inward faces, up to the landing where the counting resumed, and he felt it resume, the tower's attention settling back onto him floor by floor, the visit books, the porters, the dockets, the vast recorded world in which he was now a man carrying two crimes in a field case.

On his own floor, in his workroom, behind blood, he set the Mother on the bench in the lamplight, and its green glass took the light the way it had taken the light in a border surgery twenty one years ago, and he stood before it with his scarred hands at his sides, and the room now held, he observed, the entire estate of whatever he had become: a hundred pages of the world's ending; a chit with five words; a plait of waxed cord; a length of copper wire, worn bright; a stone that remembered a boy the world had smoothed away; and one ugly machine that knew how to give things back.

In the ledger with no pages he made the last entry of the winter, and it was one word, and it was not a finding. Findings described. This did not describe; it undertook, and he wrote it in the dark behind his eyes where the drawers could not reach, over the sound, eleven floors down, of four hundred banked fires keeping count:

Repair.

Above him, in the aerie, the wind was turning to the north. Below him, the bones faced the mountain's heart. And in between, on a bench, in a locked room, at the exact center of the tower's forty floors of reverse, the Mother stood in her sacking scars and waited, as she had waited since the border country, for the taper, and the voice in the room, and the work.
