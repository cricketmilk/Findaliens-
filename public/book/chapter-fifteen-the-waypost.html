Part Two — The Breaking
Chapter Fifteen — The Waypost

By the third western ride the journey had acquired a costume of its own: the ninety day follow, extended by his signature into a standing schedule of district inspections, four chapter houses on a winter circuit of which Harrow Fell was, on paper, merely the furthest. The tapers went with him in the field case. The second session had returned the words wax and cold, spoken in her sleep at the candle's last inch; the third had returned Brann, which had kept him company for a hundred miles, because it was the first word she had reached for that was not her own. A self coming back the way a fire takes: first the ember, then the near twigs. The order of the lighting.

The waypost at the fork of the west road kept a common room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, and he came down into it at dawn on the fourth day of the third circuit, and Grisaille was sitting at the corner table with tea.

He did not break stride. He was distantly proud of that, later, the way he had been proud of his pen before the summary band: the animal in him had already finished its arithmetic while his boots crossed the boards, and the arithmetic was simple. She was two hundred miles from the tower, at the one fork on the one road, on the one morning. There was no coincidence large enough to hold that. The meeting place was the message, delivered before either of them spoke: I know where this road goes. I have known for some time.

The innkeeper did not glance at her. The carters at the long table did not glance at her. She sat in the corner being the corner, brown and grey and still, her hands folded beside the cup with that totality of rest he had seen twice before, and he took the bench across from her because the alternative was to stand in a public room being seen not sitting.

"Magister. The tea is better than it should be, this far out."

"You are a long way from requisitions."

"Requisitions is mostly distances." She poured for him from the pot, unhurried, an administrator on a road, nothing anywhere in her posture for any watcher to keep. Then she set a folded paper beside his cup, small, quarter sheet, and returned her hand to its stillness.

He read it. It was a letter from the warden of Harrow Fell to the district office of the halls, dated eleven days prior, respectful, conscientious, troubled: a young man doing his duty, noting for the record the irregular frequency of senior visits to a closed subject in his district, and requesting guidance. It was exactly the letter Aurelius had known was coming since the second ride. It had never arrived. Across its head, in the neat stamp of the district office, ran the single word misdirected, and the file mark it should have carried was absent, and the letter that should by now have been a summons was instead lying folded by his tea, two hundred miles from every register, in the stillest hands in the Empire.

"Postal service in the western districts," Grisaille said, "has never been what it might be."

He folded the letter once more and set it down. Everything it proved, it proved in both directions at once; that was her material; he knew the craftsman now. She had shown him protection, and the protection proved surveillance, and the surveillance proved that the road he thought was his secret was a corridor she kept, and the keeping might be alliance and might be husbandry, and no instrument he owned could separate the two. A resistance conducted inside a larger patience. He looked at the possibility squarely, at the corner table, in the tallow smell, and could not resolve it, and filed it unresolved, and reached instead for the one instrument he had that she had given him herself.

"There is a star in the west," he said. "Low, in the winter dark. The catalogue calls it the Cantor's Lamp. I have been collecting its older names. Did your people have one?"

It was the first time he had seen her pause.

Not long. Not theatrical. Her pauses were not architecture, the way Ignis's were; this was a true hitch, a beat of dead air in a machine that did not otherwise idle, and when she answered, her voice did the thing that faces do when their owner declines to govern them for a moment, out of economy, or fatigue, or something she would not have named in the hearing of anyone she considered a witness.

"I was raised by the Chorus, Magister. We were not given stars."

The carters laughed at something down the room. The fire snapped. Aurelius sat with what she had said, and found that of everything the winter had shown him, the drawers, the papers, the screens, this was the sentence with the coldest floor under it. He had asked his question twelve times and received yeses and noes and one terrible vacancy, but every answer, even Moss's, had been the answer of a person who had once had a country and kept it or lost it. She was something else. Somewhere in the Concord's dormitories, the institution had raised children on catalogue names alone, and issued them a sky with no whispered floor beneath it at all, nothing to keep, nothing to lose, nothing to be asked after in an amber room, and one of those children had grown up to sit in wayside inns holding the whole paperwork of the end of the world in her folded hands.

There was no hollowing such a person. There was no old country to drain. He understood, sitting across the cooling tea, that he was looking at the Chorus's true finished product, the thing the Long hollowing was for, arrived early, made by kinder means, and that whatever moved her, and something moved her, it was not what moved Tal or Sarat or the moor. It was native to the machine. He would spend the year that followed trying, off and on, to name it, and every attempt would fail the same way, by assuming it was a wound. It was not a wound. He would learn that too late to bill anyone. It was a position.

"Why this," he said quietly. "Why any of it. The chit. The papers. The door in the question. This." He touched the folded letter. "What is it for?"

Grisaille looked at him for a moment with her brown, patient, unblinking eyes, and then she told him, in the way she told everything, flatly, without emphasis, one sentence set down on the table like a folder:

"There is a part in the cantus for a man the glamour cannot read."

The room went on being the room. Tallow, wool, carters, dawn. Aurelius heard the sentence arrive and begin, immediately, the work it had been machined to do, which was to unfold, and keep unfolding, and refuse to finish unfolding, in every direction at once.

"The cantus," he said.

"The papers you have are provisioning." She rose, unhurried, gathering her plain cloak from the bench, an administrator with distances to cover. "Provisioning is what a performance eats. It is not the performance."

"Which part," Aurelius said.

She settled the cloak at her throat with her still hands, and regarded him, and for the second time in a quarter hour he could not read what looked back, and knew only that it was, for once, looking at him from very close.

"Casting," said Grisaille, "is not my office."

Then she paid for her tea with coins counted before he had come downstairs, and was out the door into the snow light, and the corner was only a corner, and the letter was gone from beside his cup, though he had not seen her hand move at all.

He rode west with the sentence, and the sentence rode better than he did.

A part for a man the glamour cannot read. He turned it at every mile post. There was a script, then. Somewhere above the provisioning, above the annexes and the quotas, the eclipse had a dramaturgy, written as carefully as the rest, and it required, somewhere in its acts, a figure the archive could not model, and there was exactly one man in the Concord answering that description, and the script's authors knew where he ate his dinners.

Every ending he had ever read had two such parts in it. That was the shape the sentence would not stop unfolding into, through four days of fells and snow. Every salvation in every scripture the Chorus had ever harvested kept a role for the unreadable stranger, and the role was the deceiver, or the role was the redeemer, and the two were cast from the same rare stock, and the difference between them was nothing in the casting and everything in the final act. She had told him he was in the cantus. She had declined to tell him as what. And she had chosen, out of all her hours, the morning he rode to do repair, at a table on the repair road itself, to tell him, which was either a warning to a man being measured for a villain's costume, or the first briefing of an understudy, and the tea had been good, and her hands had never moved, and casting was not her office.

He reached Harrow Fell on the fourth evening. The candle stood in the west window where it had stood since the first taper, kept lit, he had learned, by the woman herself, who could not say why she did it and did it anyway, nightly, the wick trimmed with a chandler's neatness, a rite running on muscle and returning wax while its reasons made their slow way back up out of the drawers of her.

He sat in the warm room and lit the taper from her hearth, and did the work, and near the candle's last inch the woman in the chair, traveling somewhere far under, said, clearly, in a voice with more water moving beneath the ice than he had yet heard:

"The order goes widdershins. Tell Brann's boy. The order goes widdershins from the cairn."

Instruction. Not a word; a sentence; a correction, offered across eleven years of dark to a boy on a ledge with an unlearned rite, gentle, exact, unapologetic. The right way existed and was owed, and the first whole sentence of her return was her reaching for it, for him, for the fire, for the transmission the Chorus had cut, and Aurelius sat with the taper burning down in his scarred hand and let the two truths of his winter stand in the one room at last, in plain sight of each other:

Somewhere east, a script with his name in an unmarked part.

Here, west, a teacher coming back up her own dark stair, one sentence at a time, to a class that had never been dismissed.

He could not read his part. Neither could the glamour. He banked her fire, and rode for the tower in the morning, and proceeded, because the tapers had only two winters of nights left, and whatever the cantus had written him as, the order went widdershins from the cairn, and someone had to tell Brann's boy.
