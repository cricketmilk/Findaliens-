Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Five — The Table

The dinner stood in his calendar as the solstice stands in a year. Third week of the month, the seventh hour, the high chambers on the hall floor, and it had stood there for fourteen years, through border wars and plague seasons and the deaths of three archivists general, unmoved, because Ignis did not move things. Ignis arranged the world so that things did not need to be moved.

Aurelius climbed to it through the tower's evening, past the refectories and their noise, past the sealed chamber where the Inner Chorus met behind screens, and he observed himself climbing with a distant professional interest, as he had once observed subjects walk to the chair. Four days he had lived with the papers behind the blood lock. Four days of sessions kept, audits filed, letters answered, a man moving through his own life like a copyist through a fair text, and tonight was the first examination, because the woman from Harrow Fell had been read by instruments, and tonight he would be read by the man who had built the reader.

The chambers were as they had always been. That was their character and, he understood now, their function. Plain grey walls, one good lamp, a table laid for two with things of a simplicity that took decades of power to afford: bread, oil, white cheese, a dish of olives from the southern terraces, wine the color of the lamp. Nothing on the table declared anything. Everything on the table was the best of its kind in the Empire. It was the room in the tower most like its owner, and a younger Aurelius had loved it as the one place in the black stone where nothing was being recorded because nothing needed to be. He had believed that for fourteen years. He examined the belief now, turning it in the light, and set it down without a conclusion, which was itself a conclusion.

"Aurelius."

Ignis said the name the way he always said it, as a completed thought. He rose from his chair to do it, tall, gaunt, grey robed, fifty years old by the face and by the records nearer ninety, and his pleasure at the sight of his guest moved through him plainly, unperformed, and lit the room, and no enemy of Ignis's had ever survived contact with that fact. The warmth was real. Instruments would have confirmed it. The warmth had always been real. It was also, he would understand too late, at full draw. There was no resting position in the man. Fourteen years of dinners, and Aurelius never once saw the string let down.

They made a matched pair in one respect, the tower's two unaged men. Aurelius stood near fifty in every record and wore a face the middle thirties would have owned without argument, the auburn of his hair untouched by one strand of grey, and the halls had long ago filed the discrepancy under the obvious explanation, the one that was true of every Voice at this table's rank and never once of him. He let the assumption stand. It was the only lie he maintained without lifting a finger. And the man across the table, who read everything, had in fourteen years of dinners never once asked what was actually in his colleague's cup, or was not, which was, when Aurelius weighed it, the most intimate courtesy Ignis had ever paid anyone: he had noticed a face the years declined to sign, and decided that the page was not his to read. Pattern does not age. There was no register in the Empire fit to hold that answer, and no one had ever required him to file it.

Beyond the high windows, over the mountains, one of the Visionary's lanterns was crossing the early dark on its fixed road, the oldest of his works still burning: a fleet of glamour bearing vessels lofted above the weather in the charter years, too high for any wing, so that no province, however far, would be beyond the reach of the Song. Villages set their evenings by them. Children were taught to pick them from among the stars, the moving points that kept to their lanes, and to take comfort. He built them, Ignis liked to say at this table, so that no soul in the Empire would ever again be alone in the dark. Fourteen years of dinners, and it had never once occurred to Aurelius to doubt that this was the whole of the truth. It was. That was the table.

"You look tired," Ignis said, pouring. "Sit. I have had them find the last of the autumn olives, and you will eat some, and we will decide together whether the year was worth keeping."

They ate, and it was easy, and the ease was the terror.

He had come up the stairs braced as a man braces for cold water, certain that four days of knowledge must stand off him like heat off a stone. And instead the old machinery of the table took them both, years of it, the standing jokes, the smallness of the talk, the way Ignis attended to the pit of each olive as if it had earned individual consideration, and Aurelius found himself answering as he had always answered, and being listened to as he had always been listened to, and somewhere in the middle of the bread and cheese he understood, with the bottom falling quietly out of the understanding, that no performance was going to be required of him at all. The table did not need his performance. The table performed itself. He would sit here inside the oldest comfort in his life while a hundred pages of provisioning stood behind his blood lock four floors down, and both things would be entirely real, and neither would touch the other, and that was not a strategy he was executing. That was the house that had been built around him, and he had lived in it his whole tower life.

"The archive audit," Ignis said. "Voss's office had your figures. She was complimentary, which for Voss is a state bordering on song. You take that cold onto yourself twice a year, and in all these years I have never once heard you ask why it must be you." He considered an olive. "I have known men who called such a thing humility. I think it is something better. I think it is accuracy. You know what the drawers are worth because you know what they are. It would be an obscenity to send anyone who did not."

"The figures were within curve," Aurelius said. "Two racks in the Fifth want reseating."

"There. You see. Within curve." Ignis smiled at him with genuine delight. "The whole Concord asleep in its drawers, and the man who keeps them says within curve and reseats two racks. If the Song has a sound, Aurelius, I have long suspected it is that one. Not the hymns. The small true report, given exactly."

The old pleasure of being seen moved in him before he could forbid it. Fourteen years of it. Never a lie. Never once in three decades a door forced. Only this: a man who found what you already were and stood beside it and admired it aloud, until the admired thing grew toward him the way a tree grows toward the one gap of light, and every stage of your life arrived looking like your own idea. He searched, as he had been searching for four days, for a single moment of falseness anywhere in the whole span of the table, one seam, one wrong word on a level line, and there was none, and he understood that there would be none, all the way down, and that this was what the papers could not put in an annex. The machine issued warmth the way it issued robes: by grade. To the instruments it prized, this table, this lamp, this voice saying your name like a completed thought; to the harvested, the chair and the chalice and a committee's word for calm. The love was real and the love was an allotment, the ration of a valued asset, and four floors down the same machine that poured his wine was pricing the necks of a hundred thousand people who would never in their lives be worth a dinner to it. Nothing else would have held him this long. That was not a tribute to the love. It was the specification it had been issued to meet.

"You are somewhere else tonight," Ignis said, without reproach. "You are always somewhere else. I have never once held the whole of your attention; I have held more of it than anyone living, and it has made me vain. Do you know what the young ones say of you, on your own floor?" He attended to an olive, unhurried, delighted, a man who enjoyed knowing things. "That you answer the question a man meant to ask, rather than the one he managed. It unsettles the provosts wonderfully. It is also, and I tell you this because you will never once notice it yourself, how congregations begin. Men will follow a voice that is listening to something they cannot hear, Aurelius, further than they will follow bread. It is the oldest draw there is. I have built a church on it."

"The sessions." It was true. He kept to the true the way a man keeps to a cleared path in a bog. "They have been heavier lately."

"Heavier." Ignis set down his knife and gave the word the consideration he gave olive pits. "Tell me something, and take your time, because I have never yet found the bottom of this question myself. When a man lifts the same weight every day for years, and one day it grows heavy, which has changed, do you suppose? The weight, or the arm?"

"The arm carries differently as it ages."

"It does. Or." A long finger rose, the old seminar gesture, affection wrapped around it like cloth on a bell. "Or the arm has begun, at last, to feel what it lifts, which is not weakness, whatever the drill masters say. In my experience it is the beginning of the only strength worth having." He refilled both cups, unhurried. "The work you do below is the heaviest in the tower. I have said so to the screens themselves. If it has begun to weigh, then let it weigh, and bring the weight here, and we will hold it up together on this table between the bread and the olives, as we have held everything else. You are not required to be made of brass, Aurelius. That is what the drawers are for."

And there it was, served with the wine: absolution on tap, running since his first winter here, the standing offer that had kept him sane and kept him working, and he saw both halves of it at once now and could not, even now, find the border where the kindness ended and the function began. There was no border. That was the design. He drank, and let his silence pass for gratitude, which it half was, and hated the half with a flat, patient hatred that felt less like anger than like a finding.

"I hear you have taken the southern acolyte in hand," Ignis said. "The one with the steady hands."

The wine went down wrong by exactly nothing. Years of sessions had taught his body its manners.

"He cleans glass properly," Aurelius said. "That puts him ahead of two of my journeymen."

"The registrar says he follows you like iron follows a lodestone." Ignis's eyes, pale, unblinking, rested slightly past him, on whatever text they always seemed to be reading in the air behind a man's head. "I confess I hoped for it, when the intake lists came through. A gift like that boy's wants a keeper who knows what keeping costs. They are rarer than talents, such boys. The ones who hold steady while things pass through them." He ate the olive. "Be patient with him. The dormitories build a fine vessel and put nothing in it. What fills him now will be what he is."

"I'll note it in the log," Aurelius said, and Ignis laughed, the real laugh, head back, a sound this table had taught Aurelius to work for, and the two feelings stood in him side by side without touching: the pleasure of having caused it, and the cold registration that the Needle's gentlest man had just described the boy as a vessel, and the filling of him as settled, and had meant it, all of it, with love.

It came at the end, over the last of the wine, and if he had not spent four days reading annexes he would have missed it, because it was small, and nothing around it was wrong.

Ignis had been speaking of the year to come, as he did each month, the long view, the weather of the Empire, and his voice had settled into its deepest register, the one the seminars never heard. "There is so little left now, old friend. A few seasons more of patience. The provinces quiet, the granaries full, the Song sung wider every winter. I have spent a lifetime on the doorstep of something, we all have, every soul who ever did honest work in this tower, and I find lately that I think less about the work and more about the rest. The rest that comes after. There is a passage in the Song, you know it, the rest between verses, and as a boy I took it for a figure of speech, a beat of silence for the choir to breathe in. I no longer think so. I think it is the truest line in the liturgy. I think everything we do is for the rest between the verses, and we are nearly, we are so nearly at the"

He stopped.

Not a pause for effect. Ignis's pauses for effect were architecture; Aurelius could have drawn them. This was a stop, mid arch, the next word prepared and not said, and in the lamplight Ignis's gaze came down from the air behind his guest's head and settled, for three full seconds, on his own hands where they lay folded by the cup. Uncalloused hands, unaged, unmarked by the ninety years the records gave them. He looked at them as a man looks at a text in a language he used to read.

Then the sentence resumed one word downstream of wherever it had been going. "We are nearly at winter," Ignis said, "and I keep you too late in a cold room. Some things do not change." The warmth came back up his face like the lamp being turned; perhaps it had never left; there was no instrument in the room fit to say. He rose, and took Aurelius's shoulder at the door, the grip firm and dry and fond, the same grip, unchanged since the first night.

"Sleep," Ignis said. "You carry more than you say, you always have, it is the best and the least sensible thing in you. Leave some of it in the drawers where it belongs. The world is nearly ready to be kind, Aurelius. I have given my whole life to the nearly. Let an old man believe he will see the kind."

The corridor took him back in its ordinary cold. He went down the stairs counting nothing, which for him was a kind of vertigo, and stood at last on his own floor in the dark between the lamps.

Fourteen years, and no lie in any of it. He had audited the whole span now, table by table, door by door, and the finding stood past argument: he had never once been deceived. He had been found, young and strange and full of grief, by a man who saw exactly what he was and loved exactly that, and who had then, with perfect patience and a warmth no instrument could fault, arranged the light so that everything Aurelius grew toward was the harvest. You do not open a door by pushing. You stand near it, and you are quiet, and the one inside chooses the hour, and comes through of his own accord, and is grateful.

He had used those very motions on the boy, four days ago, over a cleaning cloth. He had been proud of the gentleness.

Aurelius stood in the dark of his floor with his scarred hands at his sides, and let the last of the table's warmth go out of him like heat off a stone, and entered the night's finding, in the old cold form, exact:

He could not name one lie. That was the debt. He had been loved the whole way down, and the love was real, and the love was rations, and the way was down.
