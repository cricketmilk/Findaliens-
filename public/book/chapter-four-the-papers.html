Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Four — The Papers

His workroom opened to blood and to nothing else. A door of black iron off his quarters, a lock he had grown himself from a mother batch a dozen years ago, keyed to what moved in his veins, and he had tested it against picks, against heat, against a Voice's master seals borrowed for the purpose, and the door had held, and he had permitted himself, in a tower where every record was a record, the luxury of one room with no witnesses in it.

The packet was on his desk, squared to the desk's edge.

He stood in the doorway for the space of four breaths. The door had been locked. The door was always locked; the lock did not have a forgetting in it; he had grown it himself and he knew its moods the way Sarat knew her bird's. Behind him the corridor was empty and had the settled cold of a corridor that had been empty for hours. On the desk the packet sat in a grey paper wrapper with no seal, no direction, no note, squared to the edge with a precision he recognized, because it was his own, because whoever had set it down had known that a crooked packet would say intruder and a squared one would say colleague, and had chosen what to say.

The chit had been the knock. This was the door being opened. Not his door. Theirs.

He did not touch it for a long time. He checked the room instead, every drawer, every seal, the alembic settings, the dust he kept, deliberately, in a fine film along certain sills, and the dust was unmarked, and the room had no memory of anyone, and that was itself the signature. Somewhere in this tower was a person who could pass a blood lock without waking it and cross a sealed room without touching its air, and who used that fact, that terrifying fact, to deliver mail.

Then he sat down and unwrapped the packet, because the alternative was to burn it unread, and he had known men who could have done that, and had classified them long ago among the things he was not.

The papers were copies. He established that first, from the hand, a fair copyist's hand, rapid and characterless, and the part of him that filed things filed this too: whoever they were, they had copied it themselves. No second party. No clerk of record. Hours of work at some other desk, at some other lamp, by someone to whom these pages were worth both the labor and the risk, twice over, once to make and once to give away. And one thing more, which he noticed on the third pass and then sat very still with: every sheet carried, near the binding edge, a single small fold, pressed flat, deliberate, the same fold in the same place on every page. He knew the fold the way a forger knows his own bill. He had designed it at a border bench in his last unsigned years, for a man who did not exist, and retired it the day he signed, and no living soul had ever been told what it was, and here it stood on a hundred pages of the world's ending, load bearing, in a hand that was not his. He smoothed the first one with his thumb before the knowing caught up, and left the rest alone, and added to the night's inventory a finding he could not file anywhere at all: somewhere in the Concord, his one invented lie had learned to walk, and it was telling the truth.

The first page bore a title in the dry register of the halls, and the title was Provisioning for the Final Harmony, Phased, with Annexes, and the phrase had the Chorus's whole genius in it, because a man could read it aloud in any market of the Empire and no head would turn. And past the language stood the better lock: paper in the Concord is true when an office certifies it true, and the only office that could stand behind these pages was the one they described.

He read for six hours. He did not take notes. There was no need; the failure of his memory was not among his problems, had never been among his problems; his problems ran the other way.

It was the Rending. Not the folk prophecy, not the Old Ways' murmur about the Veil that the moor grandmothers kept alive alongside the star names. An operation. Scheduled, provisioned, annexed. The pages laid it out in the bloodless grammar of supply, and the grammar was the horror, because he knew this grammar, he wrote in this grammar, and it had never once occurred to him that the end of the world would arrive in it. Requisitions. Sequencing. A production schedule for the anneal that he read three times, his own seven passes, his own season of care, multiplied through forty stills running in parallel in facilities he had never been told existed, his handwriting industrialized, his patience made into plant. An annex he read twice entered the preparation itself as a controlled instrument, the formula property of the halls, possession outside licence a theft. Theft, of the thing he had made. The array requisitions carried a contractor's stamp he knew from every crier post in the Concord, the single stark glyph of the Founder, entered against the tithes with a builder's invoice discipline: forty hills, priced and phased, the end of the world let out to the low bidder, who was also the only bidder, who had also written the tender. And the targeting annex, the one that promised endings bespoke per the archive's holdings, bore no author at all. Only a small chartered sigil in the corner of every page, a customs house he had never heard of, a stone with an open eye cut into it, billing the Chorus, line by line, for the asking. He knew that house's art by its trade name, the way every senior of the tower knew it: glamour, an old word lifted from the fell stories the art had been fed on, a grandmother's word for the casting of a seeming over a living eye. What a light does when it lies. The annex never printed the word. The annex was the word, a hundred pages of it, priced. And under both marks, on every page of both annexes, the same watermark rode in the paper itself, pressed into the sheet at the kiln: the mark of the Verdance, the one works in the Concord that grew the silverthread every casting of glamour on every side of every question ran on, seller to the halls and the Saltmoot and the border forts and, he thought with a coldness that had nowhere useful to go, to his own floor, his own instruments, his own green glass. Whichever way the age broke, the thread had already been paid for.

The last annex had no budget lines at all. One page, marked deferred, for the generation after, and it proposed, in the gentlest furniture language of the whole packet, the collar's successor: a stone seated within, grown into the skull behind the ear, unremovable by design, so that the keeping might begin at the source, before the throat shapes the word. The annex bore a single word for a title, and the word was Indwelling, and he read it four times, and closed the packet on it, and sat for a moment with both hands flat on the wrapper, in the attitude of a man holding a door shut.

And a date. The papers did not conceal the date; the papers were organized around it, every annex counting backward from it like tide tables. The Long Eclipse. A true eclipse, total, crossing the Concord and Osmara in one shadow, at the second midwinter from this one.

Every people was to receive its own ending. That was the plan's beating center, stated once, plainly, in the language of delivery: narratives of conclusion, bespoke, by population, per the archive's holdings. For the Saltmoot, the sea. Drowned cities, a tide that came and did not go out, the whole coast's oldest fear promoted to prophecy. For Osmara, the altars. The temples across the Narrow Sea had been seeded for a generation, said the annex, said it in one subordinate clause, a generation of patient priestcraft dismissed into a comma, and on the night of the shadow their gods would bleed where congregations could see. And for the Empire, for the fed and orderly and collared Empire, the Final Cadence: the Maker ending the verse, the Song closing over every head at once, each subject hearing it in the cadence of their own harvested dead.

The archive. His archive. The drawers he audited twice a year, the forty thousand held breaths at the temperature of the north, cited here as a munitions store. Per the archive's holdings. Fifty one years of a ferryman. Eleven years of a woman from Harrow Fell. Holdings.

Near the end, in an annex on doctrine, he met a sentence he knew.

The vessel may be emptied by art; it may be filled only by story.

He had seen it once before, years ago, in a memorandum he had never been meant to see, and it had frightened him then in a private, technical way, the way a man is frightened by a diagram of his own hands. Here it stood promoted. Cited, footnoted, load bearing: the finding on which the whole structure rested. His art was the emptying. The eclipse was the filling. The papers did not use the word weapon anywhere in a hundred pages, and did not need to, and he sat in his one unwatched room in the black tower with the whole of it assembled in him, pattern complete, the way he had assembled patterns his entire life, and for once in that life he would have given a great deal to be wrong, and knew, with the same instrument that had never once been wrong about a pattern, that he was not.

There was a citation he could not follow. It recurred through the annexes like a bass line: escalation per the sky ledgers, volumes nine through eleven. No copies of those. Only the citations, and around them a scheduling logic he could read from its edges: the seasons themselves were in the plan. The grey summers. The failing rains. The strange early sunsets he had been counting at the north window for years, the wrongness in the light that the almanacs did not mention. The papers mentioned it. The papers relied on it. The sky was to arrive at the eclipse already broken, already frightening, a witness prepared as carefully as the temples of Osmara, and reading that, he sat very still, because a deduction he had made as a boy and put away as unprovable had just been confirmed in a footnote by the people who had done it.

He was not surprised. He noted that he was not surprised, and noted what that said about how long he had known without permitting himself the word.

It was near dawn when he did the last thing. He took down his own tables, the astronomical ones, the honest ones, mathematics being the one register in the Empire that had never learned to lie, and he worked the eclipse himself, shadow track, totality, date. The papers were correct. Of course they were correct. And the date, which the papers gave only as a number in the imperial calendar, resolved under his pen into what it was, into the day of the year it would fall on, and he sat looking at the result for a long time.

Midwinter. The solstice. The fire night.

The one night of the year the old faith climbed to the high places. The night the beacons went up the dark, one by one, at their patient elevens, on every fell and headland the Chorus had never quite reached. Whoever had chosen the date had chosen it the way everything in the hundred pages was chosen, for maximum resonance, for the theft of an existing hour, and against the whole black weight of that he set, because it was what his mind put in front of him, one moor woman in a cold chamber, making arrangements for exactly that night. Someone will need to light the western fires at midwinter. Brann's boy knows the way up but not the order of the lighting.

Two years, less a season. Her fires and their shadow, booked for the same sky.

He wrapped the papers, and stood, and the blood lock knew him and let him to his shelves, and he set the packet where he kept the batch records from the clinic years, which was the shelf he could find in full dark, and then he did something he had not planned. He took the folded chit from his pocket, five words in a characterless hand, and set it on top of the wrapper, the knock and the door together, and stood with his scarred hand flat on both for the length of four counted seconds, which was becoming, he observed, a liturgy with a growing congregation.

Then he locked the room on it all and went to stand at the north window while the light came up wrong over the mountains, and made, in the ledger with no pages, the first entry of the rest of his life. Not a plan. He had no plan yet. An audit finding, in the form findings took when they were true past argument:

The machine was his. The occasion was theirs. And somewhere between the two, on a night two midwinters out, there was going to be a discrepancy.
