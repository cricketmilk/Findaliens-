Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Nine — The Attuned

The closure visit was his own invention. He had written it into the protocol in the first year of the sessions, over objections that it wasted a senior's travel days, and he had defended it with an argument the halls could hear: the veil memory was new work, and new work wanted inspection in the field, at thirty days, by the hand that performed it. The argument was true. He had long since learned what a true argument could be for. What the closure visit was, and had always been, was this: the rule that whoever emptied a person had to go, afterward, and look.

They sent her home early. The convalescent cell was wanted; that was the whole of the stated reason; somewhere in the tower a clerk had weighed a bed against a woman and the bed had come out ahead, and so it was the first week of winter, not spring, when Aurelius rode west with her file in his case, up out of the lowland grey and into the country of his falling.

He had not been back in decades. He had arranged his life so that there was never a reason, and the halls had obliged him, because the western foothills were nothing: sheep, peat, slate, eleven house villages strung along the fell roads like knots in old cord. The horse climbed, and the air thinned and sharpened and began to smell of wet stone and burning peat, and his body knew it before his ledgers did. The first country he was ever cold in. The grass came up beside the track, frost stiffened, clinging, and he looked at it longer than the track required. Cold grass had been the first fact of the world. It had not changed its testimony.

Harrow Fell was nine houses and a chapter house the size of a barn. The warden who came out to meet him was young, earnest, honored by the visit, and had prepared, visibly, remarks. Aurelius heard the remarks through to their end, courteously, and thanked him for them by name. The warden lost his place in them twice; people did, near him; a prepared speech rarely survived that settled elsewhere attention. And afterward the young man walked him the length of the village anyway, closer than duty wanted, the way the drawn always walked with him, telling him things no inspection had asked after: his mother's letters, the hard winter, a dream he had twice had about the fells. People emptied their pockets for the strange still man from the tower, and were embarrassed later, and could not have said why they had done it, and mostly did not regret it. Over the warden's shoulder he read the village: the woodpiles, the mended walls, the lamps already lit against the early dark. And the iron.

New iron, over two lintels. Horseshoes, both, nailed fresh this season, the nail heads still bright, and both on houses whose doors faced the cottage at the village's west end. He knew the custom. Iron over the door, for the old reasons, against the neighbors, the ones never described more exactly. The moor had a grammar for things that were present and not present, and it had reached for that grammar, quietly, with a hammer, twice.

"She has settled wonderfully," the warden was saying. "Truly. You will see. The whole village remarks on it. Such peace."

"Who leaves the milk?" Aurelius asked.

The warden blinked. On the sill of the west cottage, plainly visible down the lane, a covered jug stood catching the last light.

"Her neighbor. The widow Tam. It is only," the warden's honesty caught up with him mid sentence and he finished smaller, "kindness. They are kind people here."

Milk on the sill. Set out for the woman the way the moor had always set it out, for what walked past in the dark, for the fair folk, for whatever was owed and could not be spoken with. They had fed him at thresholds once, in this same country, in the same grammar. The village had no word for what had come back to it wearing Thessine's face, and so the old language had stepped in where the Song went silent, and had begun, gently, correctly, to treat her as one of the neighbors.

He would not put that in the file either.

She received him at her own table, in a room that was warm and clean and smelled of beeswax, and she did not know him.

He had prepared for it. Preparation, he found, was a coat in rain: technically present. She met his eyes with mild welcome, this stranger from the tower with papers to sign, and there was nothing under the welcome at all, no draft of the chamber, no shadow of the chair, and that was correct, that was the veil holding exactly as designed, and he sat down opposite her wanting, with an intensity that had no useful direction, to be anyone else on the errand.

The cottage was in order. That was the first thing the closure form asked and the easiest to certify. Fire laid, floor swept, the winter's peat in, and along the north wall, on racks he recognized as decades old, the candles. Dozens of them, dipped and cooling, in graded sizes, honey colored, the work of hands that had done it so long the doing had gone below memory, where the anneal did not reach. She followed his glance and smiled.

"I take them to the market at Ketle," she said. "They sell well. People say they burn steady."

"They always did," he said, before he could weigh it.

"Did they?" Mild interest. A hostess's interest. She turned a candle in her stained hands, considering it, and he watched her hold eleven years of craft, thirty generations of craft, a whole calendar of vigils and thresholds and fells, and feel the weight of a candle. "It's good wax," she concluded, and set it down.

He worked through the form. She answered everything, easily, the fever story sitting in her as smooth as a healed bone: the chapter house had been so good to her, the sickness had been dangerous, she was stronger now than she had any right to be, the Song be thanked. Sleep, appetite, spirits: good, good, good, and every answer true, and he wrote each one in its line with a pen that got heavier by the entry. Subject integrated. Community relations warm. He thought of the iron. No distress observed. He thought of the milk.

At the end, with the form wanting only signatures, he asked a question of his own. He had asked one in the chamber; he had asked one of the bird; it was becoming, he understood, the last unbudgeted line of his practice, the place where whatever was left of him lived.

"Midwinter is coming," he said. "The long dark. What will you do with it?"

"Sleep early, I expect." She said it comfortably, tucking a loose thread of dark hair away, a woman entirely at home in her one life. "The nights are so long up here. There's nothing to be up for."

The fire ticked. Outside, the early dusk was coming down the fell.

"There used to be fires," he said. "On the tops. Midwinter fires."

"So they say." She glanced toward the window, unbothered, faintly amused, the way one is amused by the customs of a country visited once. "Old people kept it up, I think. It's a hard climb in the dark, for a bit of light." And then, kindly, seeing his stillness and mistaking it entirely: "It must seem a strange little place, to a man from the tower. It's quiet, but it's honest. We're early to bed and the winter passes."

She would not notice, or would notice the way one notices weather in another province. He had written that sentence in a report once, about subjects in general, in the flat aggregate grammar of reports. It sat across the table from him now drinking tea, and he certified it, line by line, in his own hand, and the form was accurate. Every entry true. The sum a lie. It was the first document he had ever falsified by telling the truth on it, and he signed it twice, where the lines wanted.

He left in the last light, and at the bend where the lane met the fell road, he stopped the horse.

High on the western shoulder, small and orange against the coming dark, a fire was burning.

Wrong. He knew it was wrong before he knew how he knew, and then the knowing caught up: too low on the fell, a hundred paces below the old scorch line his eyes had found and filed on the ride in; too early in the evening; too small, a fire built by someone who had gathered fuel in daylight and hurried, not one fed patiently from a carried coal. A boy's fire. Brann's boy, who knew the way up but not the order of the lighting, keeping what he could of a thing no one had finished teaching him, alone, in the wind, on the wrong ledge, at the wrong hour, in the first of the world's last two winters.

Aurelius sat his horse in the cold and watched it, and could not have said, under any instrument in the Needle, what was in him. The fire wavered, caught, held. It was wrong in every particular and it was burning, and somewhere three hundred miles east a hundred pages of provisioning stood behind a blood lock with an annex for nights like this, and the annex had not priced this, a boy on a ledge with an unlearned rite, keeping a broken tune because the whole one had been filed in a drawer.

Behind him, a door. He turned. Thessine had come out onto her step in the cold, shawled, holding the jug of milk the widow Tam had left, and she too was looking up at the fell, at the small wrong light on the shoulder of the dark. He watched her look at it. He watched with everything he had, every instrument, the whole of the sight, hunting in her uptilted face for one flicker of the woman who had corrected him across a ledger, who had climbed four winters of that dark with a carried coal, who had made arrangements, in the last free sentence of her life, for exactly this fire.

"Pretty," she said.

Then she took the milk in off the sill, the way you do, in the cold country, with the dark coming on, and went inside, and shut the door, and after a while the man on the horse rode east, down out of the hills, with a form signed twice in his case and the fire burning wrong and unquenchable behind him, and the finding he entered that night was one line, in the old cold form, and he did not read it back:

He had made her safe from everything she had ever loved. The cure would be to make her grieve. He was going to learn to do it anyway.
