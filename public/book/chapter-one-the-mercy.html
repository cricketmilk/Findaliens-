Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter One — The Mercy

The chamber was kept at cellar temperature. Resonance keeps in cold the way meat does, and the decay curves he had been shown were correct, which had ended the argument without settling anything. The subjects sat the hour in whatever they had been brought in. The crystal was the Empire's. The shivering was theirs. In his case, under the instruments, he carried a folded blanket down to every session, and in ten winters it had never once come out.

They brought her in at the eighth hour. He entered the time in the session ledger, precise to the minute.

Subject: female, twenty five years. Province of origin: the Fourth, northern district. The census said the Fourth Province, as it said everything. The part of him that was never off, the part that counted stairs and heard lamp oil burning unevenly and always knew which corner of a room held the draft, recorded that she did not walk like the Fourth Province. She walked like high country. Short strides, weight kept over the feet. A walk for ground that might give. The part was no lover of counting. Numbers were only where lying showed first; what it hunted, waking and sleeping, was the seam between what a thing was and what it was said to be, and in forty years of this world it had never once run out of work.

He wrote the Fourth Province.

Aldric stood at the preparation bench with the chalice, exactly where the diagram in the training manual would have put him. The boy lived to the diagram. Twenty years old, a southern face, dormitory posture, and the steadiest hands Aurelius had seen in nine intakes of acolytes. He had learned the boy's name the first morning, before the hands had shown him anything. He made a point of names; the tower ran on numbers, and a name cost nothing to keep and was, most days, the only thing he could give anyone in this building that the registers would not collect.

The woman took the chair without being told to. Most of them came in past fear, into a kind of ferocious composure. He did not rank it. However they came in, begging or singing or still, they arrived as the same thing, the harvest of a century of patient engineering, driven toward this cold room their whole lives by hands they had never once been permitted to see. The charges were written as if the chair were something a person earned. The truer account he kept where no line could reach it: no one earned the chair. The chair was prepared for them, the way the crisis was prepared, and the queue, and the verse, and the only party in the room with anything to answer for sat on his side of the table. Her hands rested on her thighs. Weathered hands, dark at the creases. Soot, he thought at first. Then, no. Wax and soot both, worked in over years, the kind of stain that had stopped being stain and become skin. Under the smell of the chamber's cold and the vinegar they mopped with, she smelled faintly of beeswax.

He did not write that anywhere. There was no line for it.

"Thessine," he said. "Of Harrow Fell."

"Yes."

"You understand what this session is."

"Yes."

Most of them said more. Some begged; begging had a shape, and shapes could be managed. Some quoted the Song at him, because they believed the Song would help them, and the Song was the thing holding the door shut. That was not a fault in them. It was the machine's finest workmanship, a harvest taught to plead in the harvester's own hymns, and the cost of hearing it done belonged to his account, not theirs. She did neither. She looked at him with the settled attention of someone at a long vigil, someone for whom sitting still in a cold place at a fixed hour was practice.

He read the confirmation of charges, because the procedure required the subject to hear them, and the procedure had been written by people who understood that a thing read aloud from a ledger acquires the weight of weather. Unlicensed observance. Maintenance of beacon fires on the western fells, four consecutive winters. Instruction of minors in practices contrary to the One Song. And the naming of stars against the approved catalogue. Specifically, repeatedly, and before witnesses, the star called the Cantor's Lamp.

"Do you confirm these accounts," he said. It was not a question, and he did not give it a question's shape.

"The Lamp," she said.

He looked up.

"That is what they printed." Her voice was low and exact, a voice for saying things once. "Its name is Glede."

He knew the word. A live coal. It was the old tongue of the uplands, where a fire banked at night and carried to the next hearth in an iron box was a glede, a thing that looked spent and was not. In the approved catalogue the star was the Cantor's Lamp, four hundred years old, named for a functionary. The name she had said was older than the Empire that renamed it, and she had corrected him gently, without apology, the way a woman corrects a child's grip on a spoon. Because the right way existed, and was owed.

He noted that she had not argued the charges. Only the name.

"Noted," he said, and wrote confirmed.

There was one more line in the preliminary section, an open line, statements of the subject, if any, and he asked it in the manual's words. "Is there anything you wish recorded."

She considered the question with the same economy she seemed to bring to everything, spending words the way moor households spent lamp oil.

"Someone will need to light the western fires at midwinter," she said. "Brann's boy knows the way up but not the order of the lighting. Tell whoever keeps the lists."

He held his pen still over the ledger for a moment.

She had understood the charges. She had walked past the projection cradle on the way to the chair, and whatever the moor villages did or did not know about the Needle, he had watched her look at the array with those vigil eyes and understand exactly what it was, which was: this is the place where they will take it out of me. And her statement for the record was a staffing concern. Not mercy for herself. Continuity of the fires.

There was no line for that either. He wrote: subject made a request regarding local custom; noted; no action.

"Aldric," he said.

The boy brought the chalice.

The distillate in the chalice had cost a season. Seven passes, each one a month of measuring, and a single error in the sixth pass would have ruined the batch invisibly; the sixth showed nothing, and the seventh showed what the sixth had done. His hands had done the work, and his hands bore the record of it, the fine white lines and the quicksalt scars, and he could have recited the batch's history as the woman across from him could apparently recite the true names of stars.

In the registers, the distillate was designated by number. Nobody used the number. Everyone in the Needle, from the Voices down to the boy holding the chalice level and steady, called it what his own first monograph had called it, a word carried out of a surgery in a border town, back when the thing itself was something else. In those years it was the answer for a widow who could not stop rehearsing her husband's last hour, for a Verse taker who wept at the sight of his own children. A physician named Marrow had held the first vial up against the lamplight and said, with professional satisfaction and no irony at all: this will be a mercy.

They called it the anneal. Mercy was her word, and the registers do not requisition mercy; the word that stuck was his own, out of his first monograph, a smith's word carried into the soul's glasshouse: what a maker does to metal grown too hard to work, heat and hold and slow cooling, until the temper lets go and the material will take the tool again. He had meant it as a description. The halls had kept it as an instruction. She had named a kindness. He had named a workability, and the tower spent the next sixteen years on the difference.

It had no taste. Seven distillations remove everything, including cruelty. She drank, unhurried, to the bottom, as she had done every other thing in that room. She set the chalice back into Aldric's hands with a small nod. She thanked the boy, Aurelius noted. The boy, not him. The pouring had taken four seconds, and he counted them, because counting was a place to put his attention, and his attention needed a place that was not her face.

The anneal is not violence. He had written that sentence himself, in the early monographs, and it was true, and he had spent his whole tower service learning what a sentence can be true and still be. It does not break anything. It loosens. It takes nothing; nothing in the vial can take; taking is the array's office, and the array has no office at all in a soul still holding its grip. The self holds its contents like a fist gripping sand. The figure was Marrow's, from the clinic days. The anneal simply persuades the fist that it is tired.

He watched it happen. He always watched. That was not in the procedure. The procedure required only the pupil checks at set intervals, and he did those, and between them he watched, because somewhere in an old stratum of himself, under the ledgers and the batch numbers, he held the position that if this was going to be done, then someone whose hands had built it should have to see it, every time, all the way.

Her shoulders came down first. Then the precision went out of her posture, the stillness of the vigil slackening into the ordinary slump of a tired woman in a chair. The composure stayed longest, because it was not a performance over fear. It was structure, and structure went last. At the third pupil check her eyes had gone wide and calm, and she looked around the chamber with mild interest, as if she had just arrived, as if the room were a place she had heard described once by someone she had no reason to distrust.

"Warm," she said, to no one.

The chamber was at cellar temperature. He wrote the word in the observation line, and the time.

The stone had been taken from her collar at intake, upstairs, by other hands. It rode now in the projection cradle at the center of the array: a dark crystal in a brass housing, scratched along one face where she had worn it against her breastbone for eleven years. The regulation chain had been replaced at some point with a plait of waxed cord, which was an infraction in itself, the kind the chapter houses had stopped bothering to prosecute. People did things to the stones. They decorated them, spoke to them, tucked flowers behind the brass. It cost the resonance nothing, and the stone kept the speaking along with everything else, every endearment entered into the very record it thickened, so that the more a stone was loved, the richer the harvest it was quietly becoming. The Chorus had learned that a device that can be loved is worn without complaint, and it had learned the lesson under the lesson and printed it nowhere: the love was not wasted on the device. It was collected by it. A whole Empire garlanding the instrument that was emptying it, and the garlands improving the draw.

Eleven years of her rode in it. That was what the drawers upstairs held, what the whole black tower held, floor over floor over floor. Not information. Impressions. The discipline was strict about the word: the resonance of a soul recorded against crystal, day over day, as weather records itself in the rings of a tree. Her seasons on the fells. Her fires. The order of the lighting, which Brann's boy did not know.

The array added nothing.

That was the elegant part. It was also the unforgivable one, and the work was his. No foreign story was forced in. None could be. The vessel refuses, and forty years of Chorus alchemy had broken against that refusal before he came. The array only took what the stone held, her own life, her own voice, her own fires, and returned it to her with the weights changed. Every impression played back true and slightly wrong. The fells at midwinter, seen as if by a stranger who found them cold and far and small. Her grandmother's voice reciting the order of the lighting, rendered exactly, at the emotional pitch of a clerk reading a bill of lading. Nothing taken. Nothing added. Everything priced again, one memory at a time, at the new rate, which was nothing.

The vessel may be emptied by art. He had read that sentence in a memorandum he was never meant to see, and had recognized, with the particular nausea of a man meeting his own work in a stranger's handwriting, the mechanism it described.

He engaged the array. Silverthread came alive along the chamber walls and drank the lamplight down to brown, and the air went sweet at the back of the tongue, as air goes before close lightning, and the quicksalt scars in his hands woke and itched, the body of the operator counted into the circuit whether the diagrams admitted it or not. His ears clicked twice, the chamber pressure stepping down a stair that was not there, and copper arrived at the back of his throat where the sweetness had been. The scars stood up in ridges. They always did. He no longer looked. The chamber held only the mouth of the instrument. The body lay below: past the fourth floor the silverthread veins gathered and dropped through the tower's cold heart to the wellhouse, three vaulted floors of racked thread standing in chased air, drinking the mountain's river to keep its work cold, and when an array came alive anywhere in the Needle the whole tower knew it: a dimming in the lamps, a change in the pipes. Every session was billed below, in river water and winter air. He had read the invoices. Nobody else had ever asked to. In the cradle the stone lit from within, faintly, like an ember answering a breath.

For fifty minutes the chamber held three sounds: the low chord of the array, the scratch of his pen at the interval checks, and the woman's breathing, which stayed even throughout, because nothing frightening was happening to her. That was the drug's one kindness. He heard the breathing as he heard all rhythm, figure before flesh, a slow count laid against the array's chord, and did what forty years had taught him to do with that hearing, which was correct it: not music. Not pattern. A woman, breathing. She sat with her eyes open, watching her own life arrive and arrive and arrive, each moment of it perfectly familiar and perfectly light. If any part of her understood what was being done and screamed against it, some deep unlisted stratum, a glede under the ash, the instruments did not record it. The instruments recorded everything.

He performed the checks. He filled the lines. Half an hour in, his hand found the length of copper wire in his coat pocket, and he let it stay there, turning it, a small hidden motion, his one unlisted instrument.

The verification protocol was the shortest section of the procedure and the only one Aurelius had written entirely himself. The Chorus's own drafters had proposed doctrinal questions. Recitations, catechism, keeping tune. He had struck them out. Doctrine could be performed. What could not be performed was cost. He did not ask the subject what was true. He asked what things were worth, and listened to the arithmetic.

He kept his voice level, procedural, the register in which nothing important has ever been said.

"What is the name of the star," he said, "in the west, low, in the winter dark. The bright one."

"The Cantor's Lamp," she said.

Her voice was pleasant. It was her same low voice, with nothing under it. A room where the furniture stands in its places and the hearth is cold.

"Some call it something else," he said. There was no line on the form for the sentence, and he said it anyway.

She considered it, mildly, the way one considers a question about a town one has never visited.

"Old people, maybe," she said. "Names change." Then, with a small courteous effort, like a hostess offering the last of the tea: "The Lamp is prettier."

Behind him, at the bench, a sound. Barely a sound. Glass on wood. The chalice set down half a beat early, a fraction hard. Aldric's steady hands had landed something wrong for the first time in nine months. Under it, at the threshold of hearing, in southern vowels the dormitories had flattened, came one word, escaped rather than spoken, as steam escapes a sealed vessel at the one flaw in its seam.

"Theft."

Aurelius did not turn.

"Note the time," he said, "in the ledger."

His own voice came out very short and very precise. He heard it do that, and made no correction.

She would sleep now, half a day and a night, and wake with the cover story already grown in around the gap, closed and not healed. A fever, a dangerous one, taken at the chapter house in time. A kindness of the Song. A long convalescence, to explain the tender blankness where the weight of things had been. The drafters called this the veil memory, which he had always found obscene in its accuracy. In the spring they would send her home to Harrow Fell, calm, correct, released. Attuned. The word would travel ahead of her, and her neighbors would speak to her a little more slowly than before, and be ashamed without knowing of what, and the fires on the western fells would go unlit at midwinter, or be lit in the wrong order by Brann's boy, which she would not notice, or would notice the way one notices weather in another province.

Her stone went back to the archive in its brass housing, into a drawer with her name and her province and her years on a card in a small brass frame. He carried it up himself.

The archive at night held the temperature of the deep fells, and a silence with a floor to it. Under it, at the floor of hearing, lay the sleeping resonance of forty thousand recorded lives, a sound he had stopped being able to unhear years ago. He slid the drawer shut on eleven years of her and stood with his scarred hand flat on the brass for the length of time it takes to count four seconds, which was, if anyone had asked him, which no one ever had, the entire liturgy of whatever faith he could still be said to keep.

In the session ledger, in the final line, condition of subject at close, he had written what the protocol's vocabulary offered him: calm.

The word had been chosen by a committee. He had sat on it. He remembered the meeting. The long table, the tea going cold, Ignis at the far end, attending to each speaker in turn as if the meeting were a meal he was hosting, asking in that unhurried way of his whether anyone present could name a single respect in which the subjects were not, demonstrably, at peace. Nobody had answered him. Nobody had needed to. It took Aurelius years to name what unsettled him in that warmth, and when he named it he entered it where the dangerous findings went: the warmth had no dial. One temperature, for a cooling tea or a burning province. He titrated everything he touched, and there was no entry in any book of his for a fervor that could not be turned down. At the time he filed the observation as uncharitable. He would live to file it differently.

He had not been able to. That was the design.

He took up his case with the folded blanket he had not used, and put out the chamber lamp, and climbed toward the top of the black tower. Twelve flights, and he counted them. At the crown of the Needle was the aerie. Wind, whitewashed stone, the smell of raptors, and a creature the color of banked coals sitting unhooded in the dark, who turned on him one amber eye that had never once needed anything explained to it.

He sat down on the cold step below her perch. After a while, in the manner of a man observing a formality that has outlived every other part of its ceremony, he took the folded blanket out of the case at last, and laid it over his own knees.

"Glede," he said to the bird, testing the weight of the word. "That was the name."

Cinder said nothing, in the third of her silences, the one she kept for the rare occasions when he was present with her and not wrong about anything. It was the only silence in the Needle that did not feel like listening. Far below them both, in a cold drawer, a stone that had spent eleven years against a keeper's breastbone held its impressions in the dark. The fells. The winters. The order of the lighting. Banked, the way the uplands banked a fire.

Looking spent. And not.
