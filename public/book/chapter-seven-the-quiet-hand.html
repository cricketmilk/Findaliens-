Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Seven — The Quiet Hand

No one came onto his floor unnoticed. In all his tower years, no one had. It was not vigilance; vigilance tires; it was simply that the floor was a pattern and he lived inside the pattern, and a person entering it was a change of figure, and changes of figure arrived in him the way cold arrives in a tooth. The couriers knew to knock the doorframe. The journeymen moved in his awareness like weather fronts, tracked from three rooms out. He kept the fact the way other men kept faith, privately, as proof of something. The floor returned the strangeness with interest. His journeymen waited out his silences, and had stopped explaining to new intakes what the waiting was for; a man learned, or transferred. Two clerks in all his years had requested the transfer, citing, in the space for reasons, a thing neither could name. The ones who stayed, stayed the way iron stays near a lodestone, and one of them, he knew perfectly well, kept a notebook of sentences he had not known he had said aloud. And on the ninth day after the mews he looked up from the bench and a woman was standing inside his doorway with a paper folder in her hands, and he had no idea how long she had been there.

"Magister," she said. "A moment, for requisitions."

Everything about her had been seen before. That was the only way he could put it to himself afterward, and he tried many ways. Brown hair going grey the way brown hair goes grey. Administrator's robes of middling rank, the folder held as ten thousand clerks in the tower held folders. A face made entirely of features that occurred elsewhere. She stood with a stillness he noticed twice, once as ordinary patience, and once, a breath later, as the fact that her hands did not move at all, not a finger, not the paper, nothing, a stillness of the kind he had otherwise seen only in Cinder, and in the hollowed.

"Requisitions," he said.

"A reconciliation, before the winter close." She crossed to the bench and laid the folder open before him, and her step made the amount of sound that steps make. "Your floor's quarter. Reagent draws against logged output. The certifying line wants a senior hand."

It was the dullest paper in the Empire. Quicksalt by weight, silverthread by ell, one water by sealed flask, his floor's distillations logged and numbered down the facing column, and he read it in one pass, and certified it in his mind before his pen was in his hand, because it was correct, his floor was always correct, and he was reaching for the pen when the pattern arrived.

The folder held the Concord totals. Printed small, in the summary band across the folder's bottom edge, where totals ride on every requisition sheet in the tower, present by format, meant to be ignored. Aggregate draw, all facilities, of the reagents particular to the seventh distillation. He knew his floor's appetite to the dram. He knew, because he had written the process, that no other floor in the Needle performed it. And the aggregate stood at a figure his floor could not have consumed in six years.

The number sat in the summary band with the patience of print. It reconciled, somewhere, against facilities that had no names in any register he was cleared to read. Forty stills, said the papers behind his blood lock, in their bloodless grammar. Here was their grocery bill, riding the bottom edge of a clerk's winter reconciliation, in the hands of a woman watching him read it.

His face did what years of sessions had trained it to do, which was nothing. He certified his floor's column. His pen was level. He was pleased with his pen, distantly, as one is pleased with a well made tool doing its work far away.

"Thank you." She recovered the folder without hurry. And then, in the same register, the register of buttons counted and inkwells filled: "For completeness, Magister, one question. Is there any use of the seventh distillation that would not appear in your registers?"

The question was humble, procedural, and perfectly made. He heard all of it at once, as she must have known he would. Answer no, and he certified, for the record, in the tower's hearing, that the aggregate in the summary band described someone else's secret and not his. Answer anything else, and he opened a door in front of a requisitions clerk that a hundred pages behind his blood lock said must stay shut. And under both answers, quiet as the summary band, the question's real body: do you know. I am asking whether you know.

Or it was a completeness question, of the kind asked forty times a day, by clerks, for winter files.

"Not in my registers," he said. "No."

It was exactly true. His registers were his floor's. He heard himself deliver it in his session voice, level, procedural, and he watched her receive it, and her face did what his had done, which was nothing, and for one full second the two of them stood on either side of the dullest paper in the Empire doing nothing at each other with a precision that had taken, on his side, a career to build, and on hers, he suspected, a great deal longer.

She closed the folder. At the door, already half a pattern again, already becoming the corridor, she paused as clerks pause when a pleasantry is owed to rank.

"Most discrepancies are clerical," she said. "The instructive ones reconcile."

Then the doorway held nothing but the doorway.

Her name was Grisaille. It took him two days to establish even that, which was itself a finding, in a tower where names attached to faces as cards attach to drawers.

Requisitions oversight, hall floor, a rank that supervised nothing and touched everything. Her service record ran smooth, postings and reconciliations and commendations of the third class, thirty one years of it without one edge to catch a nail on. He read it twice. It was the record of no one. It was what a service record would look like if a careful person had asked: what does a record look like when nobody has ever needed to look at it, and then built that, year by patient year, the way he had once built a lock. The register's cover was worn soft at one corner, a place his own thumb never fell: other hands came to these pages, and were not written down either.

And the first line was wrong. Every record in the tower began with a province. Intake, origin, the census line, the Empire's first question about anyone. Hers began with the Chorus. Ward of the chapter schools, dormitory raised, no province of record, and he sat with the personnel register open in the lamplight and understood that he was reading the file of a person who had never, not once, existed outside the institution, and that the institution had looked at that fact for thirty one years and filed it as unremarkable.

He compared hands. Of course he compared hands. The certifying slips in her file against the chit in his pocket, five words, the star has two names, and the folder's docket lines against the fair copies behind his blood lock, and the comparison returned the one result he should have foreseen and had not: nothing. The tower's schools taught a single hand to every dormitory child, drilled it until the child's own wrist was gone from it. Ten thousand clerks wrote the characterless hand. Anonymity was not her talent. It was the institution's issue, standard, like the robes. The Chorus had spent a century building a class of people with no province, no wrist, no edges, and somewhere inside that class, wearing it, was whoever had passed his blood lock like weather, and the class could not be searched for her, because the class had been designed, from the first dormitory forward, to have no her in it.

He should have been able to place her face. That was the thing he came back to, the ninth night, the tenth, turning the wire. His memory did not fail. It was not among his problems; his problems ran the other way; forty years of this world stood in him complete, retrievable, indexed, and when he went looking for her in it he found what an archivist finds in a serviced drawer. The sense of an object recently present. A clean space its exact shape.

She had been somewhere in his forty years. He could feel the socket of it. And it was empty, and he had no way to know whether time had done that, or she had, and the second possibility should have been absurd, and he no longer had the papers it would take to call anything absurd.

Three readings, he wrote, on no page, in the analyst's form, because the analyst's form was the handhold he had.

A test. The halls suspected the papers had moved; the woman was the instrument sent to watch him read a number; his face had held, or had not, and the result was filed already, somewhere, in a drawer he would meet later. It fit every fact.

A warning. Someone inside wanted him to see the forty stills with his own eyes, on paper he could not be hanged for reading, and to know that his registers, his beautiful registers, correct to the dram for fourteen years, were a wall he had built himself, one certified quarter at a time, around the word no. It fit every fact.

A recruitment. The chit, the packet, the folder: three deliveries, each one larger, each one closer, a hand extending itself in stages toward a man it had studied long enough to know that he could not be summoned, could not be pushed, could only be shown true things and left alone with what he was. It fit every fact.

He sat with the three of them until the lamp burned down, and they would not resolve, and he understood at last, with something that was not quite admiration and not quite fear and had no register of its own, that the irresolution was not a failure of his reading. It was the composition. Whoever she was, she had written a figure that played all three ways at once and cost her nothing in any of them, and he knew of only one other craftsman in the tower who worked in that material, and he had eaten that man's olives four nights ago.

The instructive ones reconcile. He turned the sentence the way he turned the wire. His registers reconciled. His floor, his sessions, his audits, his whole recorded life in this tower reconciled, quarter after certified quarter.

So did hers.

He put out the lamp, and stood a while in the dark of his floor, in the changed pattern of it, and made the night's entry where no page would hold it: that somewhere in the Concord's ocean of paper, his record and hers were the two that had no edges to catch a nail on. That perhaps that was what she had come to show him, with her folder and her stillness and her one humble question. Not the forty stills. The kinship.

A person could be a blank because there was nothing there, he thought. Or a person could be a blank the way his workroom was a blank, because of what was behind the door.

He did not know which she was. He knew, now, which he was. It was a beginning, of something, and he declined, in the old way, in the dark, to name it.
