Part Two — The Breaking
Chapter Seventeen — The Rehearsal

The circular came down in the first week of true spring, on paper the color of cream, which meant ceremony: the halls announced a new cycle of observances for the coming year, to be called the Cadence vigils, in preparation for what the circular named, with the Chorus's genius for saying enormous things in furniture language, the season of the rest. There would be vigils at every chapter house, monthly, swelling toward midwinter. There would be new settings of the old verses, recovered by the archivists and approved for the whole Concord. And the first vigil would be sung at the Needle itself, before the assembled seniors and the screens, by a voice the circular did not need to introduce, because there was exactly one voice in the Empire that needed no introducing.

The Founder announced that he too would attend, in his own criers' words, to keep an eye on the robes for the people. The halls, which had issued his invitation a month before, let the announcement stand. That was the whole arrangement, in one line, for anyone who knew how to read a line.

On the night he arrived late and loudly, the stark glyph at his throat where every other breast wore the Song, and took a seat apart from the Voices with a reluctance visible from the back rows, and was watched more hungrily than the stage. Aurelius, four rows back, read the choreography. The late arrival: cleared with the prefects that morning. The seat apart: held empty by name. The reluctance: rehearsed. The one free man in a hall of grey, performed nightly, to the exact tolerances the screens permitted, for an audience that needed one to exist. The crowds cheered the cage's one painted bird, and called it the sky.

Veyra Serrat was coming to the tower.

Aurelius read the circular twice at his bench and filed what it was under both of its names. In the written register: liturgy, a festival year, an honor for the districts. In the register with no pages: the narrative sphere spinning up, seven seasons out, on schedule, per the annexes. The Empire was to spend the year before the eclipse being taught, gently, monthly, in its own beloved music, that verses end. That the ending of a verse is not a dying but a rest. That the rest is also the Song. By the far midwinter, when the sky went out, there would not be a soul under the Concord's roof who had not already rehearsed the theology of it, and the papers behind his blood lock had a line for this too, he remembered it exactly, delivery narratives, prepared populations, see annex nine, and annex nine had been about music, and he had read it in that first six hours and thought: hymns. He had not thought: her.

The great hall of the Needle was dressed for it in grey and whitewax, and the seniors sat in their ranks, and the seven screens stood lit at the far curve with their glow arranged, and Aurelius, four rows back among the alchemical faculty, watched the Empire's narrative machine perform its first public rehearsal for the end of the world, and found it beautiful, which was the finding he trusted least and recorded most carefully.

A child sang first. That was the pageant's shape, established across forty years of festivals: a chosen child, alone, raised on a small platform, singing the opening verse unaccompanied while the hall knelt into the posture the liturgy called the listening. The child was perhaps nine, scrubbed, terrified, note perfect, a dormitory child with a province somewhere behind its vowels, and four hundred of the Concord's most senior servants went down onto their knees around Aurelius in one soft grey motion, heads inclined, eyes down, rehearsed since their own childhoods, and he knelt among them, one beat late, and watched what a kneeling hall looks like from inside, and thought of pageants in market towns, and of a boy at a basin, and of how many years a reflex takes to install, and the answer was: one generation, if you start them at nine.

Then Veyra sang the new setting, and he understood, within eight bars, everything the annex had been for.

She was smaller than the broadsheets made her, fen pale under the stage color, river dark hair dressed plain for the liturgy, and she stood on the platform like a woman who had been looked at for a living since girlhood, perfectly still, angled slightly toward the doors, and her voice filled the stone hall the way water fills a lock, evenly, irresistibly, from the bottom. The setting was built on the old rest verse, the one Ignis had reached for at the table in the winter, the rest between verses is also the Song, and it did with that line what the whole century had done with everything: took a figure of comfort and turned it, by pure musical argument, one degree at a time, into a promise. The verse ends, sang the most trusted voice in the Empire, to four hundred kneeling servants and every basin in the Concord carrying it outward to the market squares. The verse ends, and the rest is not silence, and you need not fear the dark between the singing. She sang it the way truth sounds, and the hall wept, senior men, robed women, the whole grey machinery moved to real tears by real beauty in the service of a scheduled lie, and Aurelius knelt in the fourth row with his eyes down and did the only thing he had ever done in a kneeling crowd.

He counted.

The weeping came in threes. That was the first wrong figure. Grief in a hall moves like weather, patchy, local, contagious by neighborhood; this came in threes, spaced, a sob from the left ranks, answered two beats later from the right, answered again from the back, metronomic, seeding the rooms of the hall the way a claque seeds laughter, and once he had the pattern he could not lose it: nine voices, placed, working the four hundred like bellows working a fire. Rented grief, priming real grief. The oldest trick in the fairs, running at full depth inside the Needle's own sanctum, on the Needle's own people, because the machine did not exempt its servants from the machine, and had never needed to, and he knelt there hearing the ratchet work and thought, with a coldness that was almost admiration: they are rehearsing us first.

In the shuffle of rising afterward, two prefects of ceremonies passed behind him, heads together, wet faced, exalted. "The threes carried beautifully tonight," one murmured, in the tone of a stagehand pleased with his rigging, and the other agreed, warmly, and both men wiped their eyes, and neither of them fell down dead of the contradiction, and that, Aurelius understood, was the whole civic achievement of the age in one overheard sentence: they knew the grief was rented, and wept it anyway, and felt, wiping it, that they had worshipped.

And then he saw that he was not the only one counting.

Veyra had finished the verse and stood in the held silence before the applause, eyes down, the performer's humility, exact. But her stillness had changed its quality. He knew stillness; it was his life's one native subject; and hers had gone from the composed stillness of craft to the total stillness of an animal that has heard something in the grass, and when the applause came, seeded in the same threes, swelling on the same worked rhythm, he watched her listen to it. Not receive it. Listen to it. Her chin came up a fraction, and her eyes moved, left ranks, right ranks, back, tracking the plants at the exact two beat interval, and something crossed her painted face that no one in that hall was equipped to read except possibly the one man in the fourth row who had spent his whole life watching people discover a pattern that unmade their floor.

She had heard it. Perhaps not for the first time; perhaps for the first time she had let herself count; and the count was not coming out in adoration's favor, and she stood in the wash of four hundred pairs of hands taking her applause like a woman taking soundings in bad water.

They were presented afterward, in the long gallery, the visiting voice and the ranking faculty, a receiving line of grey courtesy. Her hand, when it came to his turn, was cool and callused in a way the broadsheets never mentioned, boatwoman's calluses, old ones, at the base of the fingers where poles and nets leave them, and he held it the customary second and said, because his mouth sometimes conducted its own policy:

"You didn't need the nine."

Her eyes came up fast. Up close they were fen grey, and tired under the paint, and very, very quick.

"The nine," she said.

"In the hall. Three ranks, two beat offsets." He should have stopped. He did not stop; the older part had the floor. "The setting carries on its own weight. Whoever placed them wasted the fee."

The line was moving; a prefect was already leaning in with the next introduction; there were perhaps two seconds left in the world where this conversation could exist, and Veyra of the nine bridges, the most heard voice in the Concord, used them. Her hand tightened on his, a fraction, boatwoman firm, and she said, low and fast and in an accent suddenly a degree wetter than her stage voice had ever once permitted:

"You counted them."

"I count everything."

"So do I," she said, and the line moved, and her face resumed its office, and she was gracious at the next presentation, and the next, all the way down the grey gallery, and did not look at him again, and did not need to.

Vex found him at the wine, which was where Vex found everyone. The rolls carried his office at full length, the Vexillary, bearer of the standard before the Empire's assembled legions, and the halls had worn the title down to Vex within a season, and he had kept the wearing, delighted, because a name that sounds like a flaw is underestimated at every table that matters.

He arrived the way weather arrives in the south, warm and total, a big florid man in velvet the color of church windows, rings to the knuckle, and he had Aurelius's elbow in one hand and a story going before the introduction had properly landed, his voice pitched for the room, his laugh arriving early, at his own material, twice. "Magister, Magister, the alchemist himself, no, don't protest, I know your face from the appropriations gallery, I never forget a man who looks that unhappy about money. Aurelius. You'll permit me. I have wanted the acquaintance for an age." The hand moved from elbow to shoulder, calibrated, fond. "Tell me honestly, between professionals, what did you make of my vigil? You may be exact. I am told you are always exact. I collect exactness. I married two of them."

"The setting was effective," Aurelius said.

"Effective." Vex tasted the word with delight. "Effective! There, you see, that is worth forty bravos from the districts. It was, wasn't it. It was effective." He drank, rings clicking on the cup, and leaned confidential, gallery volume, the performance of intimacy. "I'll tell you a secret about effect, Magister, from a former life. I ran a resurrection act in the southern fairs, before the robes, oh yes, look at me, you can still see the tent under all this velvet if you squint. Drowned girl, three nights a week, packed benches, wept themselves sick. And you know the whole secret of a resurrection? It is not the rising." The rings stopped clicking. "The crowd does not come to see her live, Magister. They come to see her dead first. No corpse, no miracle. Whatever else we economized, and we economized everything, we never once stinted the corpse."

And then it happened, the thing the canon of the man could not have prepared anyone for who had not been told: the noise fell out of his voice. Between one breath and the next the showman was simply absent, and something older and flatter and very tired looked out of the florid face and said, at a volume that reached exactly one person, in the tone of a man reading a manifest:

"They will need a devil by winter, and the short list is short."

Then the laugh came down over it like a curtain, enormous, head back, a hand spanking Aurelius's shoulder as if the two of them had just shared something bawdy. "But listen to me! Shop talk at a festival, my wives were right about me, both of them, whatever they tell you. Come to the south gallery, Magister, there are people who will pretend to be delighted by you, no, I insist, I have promised the prefect an alchemist and you are the least dead one I know." And he was moving, voice restored, room restored, a big warm tide going out and taking the company with it, and Aurelius stood a moment by the wine with his shoulder ringing and the sentence sitting in him where it had been placed, with a showman's precision, under the heart.

He saw Orn once more that night, in the corridor behind the great hall, where the screens' private passage met the public gallery and the light was bad and the stone was cold and no one lingered. The Founder was coming out from behind the seventh screen, and for a moment, before the mask came up, Aurelius saw the other face.

It was not the face that declaimed from crier posts and denounced the grey robes and sold the Far Shore in three tiers. It was the face of a man who had just finished a meeting about freight. Short, cold, transactional. The Founder's private register, the one the crowds never heard, speaking in the grey language he publicly mocked, to a clerk who was already nodding, already withdrawing, already carrying whatever instruction had just been issued to whatever office would make it lawful. The clerk passed Aurelius in the corridor with his head down and his hands full of paper, and the Founder looked up, and the mask came on, and the mask was the mask, enormous, quotable, warm with the warmth of a man being fed something he had not earned.

"Magister! The alchemist! I saw you in the fourth row, you have the look of a man who hates ceremony. I am a student of looks. I have built an engine works on them." The laugh arrived, too early, before the punchline, and the hand came out, rings and warmth, and Aurelius took it because the hand was there and because refusing it would have been a data point. "Tell me honestly, and you may be exact, I am told you are always exact. The vigil. Did it land? I have twenty thousand crowns of crier time riding on the answer, and I would rather hear it from a man who hates ceremony than from the committee that designed it."

"It was effective," Aurelius said.

"Effective. He says effective." The Founder turned to an audience that was not there, playing the corridor like a stage, and the performance was flawless, and underneath it, for one further breath, Aurelius caught the other thing. Not the private register. The thing under the private register, the thing the private register was built to manage: a man who had been running on the vitality draughts of his own formulation for two decades, who had children named for him across several households and one who had renounced his name, who told audiences the renounced child was dead in the present tense, and who was, at this hour, in this cold corridor, behind the rhetoric and the engines and the mask, simply tired. Not guilty. Not conflicted. Tired, the way freight is tired after a long haul, and the haul was the persona, and the persona had been running for thirty years, and the man beneath it had been worn smooth by the wearing. There was nothing under the Founder. There had never been anything under the Founder. There was a man who had built an empire on the one talent the age rewarded, which was the ability to be the loudest voice in the room, and the voice had eaten everything else, and the man knew it, and did not care, because caring was not in his inventory.

"Effective!" The Founder released his hand, delighted, performing delight, genuinely delighted and performing the genuine delight at the same time, because the two had long since become the same thing. "I will quote you, Magister. I quote everyone. It is the secret of my success." And he was gone, down the corridor, into the private passage, behind the seventh screen, where whatever freight meeting had just concluded was already being priced into the readiness curves, and Aurelius stood alone in the cold and filed him, at last, under the heading he had been building for two years: a man with no floor and no refusal and no self to reckon with, who would survive the age because survival was the only thing he had ever been good at, and the only thing he had ever been good at was inventory.

The audit, that night, ran long, and came to order around four findings.

The Empire had begun rehearsing its own funeral. Monthly now, swelling, in its best loved voice, with the grief seeded in advance and the theology installed a generation deep, and the whole grey sanctum of the Needle had wept on cue without knowing there was a cue, and this would run until the shadow, and it would work. That was one.

The borrowed face had counted the rent in her own applause, in the Needle's great hall, and had said so do I to a stranger with two seconds on the clock. Whatever began there, it had begun; he knew the sound of a floor unmaking; her thread was lit and burning toward the account under her luck, and when it arrived there she would be either the narrative sphere's most catastrophic defection or its most efficiently recaptured asset, and nothing in between. That was two.

The man behind the seventh screen had no floor under him and never had. The permitted rebel was a product, manufactured by the same house that manufactured everything, and the product was perfect, and the product was empty, and a man with nothing under him is a man who cannot be reached by any argument the resistance possesses. The Chorus had seven Voices. One of them was a bill of works with no self to reckon with, and the bill of works would survive the age because bills of works always survive, and the finding was colder than anything he had filed on Vex, because Vex at least had the crack. Orn had no crack. Orn had inventory. That was three.

And the fourth he filed last, in the pocket of the ledger where he kept the winter's strangest inventory, beside a chit and a plait of cord: the coward who owned the Rending's voice had crossed a crowded gallery to warn the leading candidate for its corpse. Warning or trap or both in sequence; the composition would not resolve; the compositions never resolved now; he was learning to work inside irresolution the way the fens worked inside fog. But he had heard the sentence under the sentence, the one the resurrection act had been built to deliver, and he entered it plainly, because plainness was all he had left at that hour:

The cantus's second act needed its miracle. The miracle needed its corpse. And the music director, who had never once stinted the corpse, was frightened enough of his own production to go leaking casting notes at the wine.

He filed Vex, after consideration, under assets.
