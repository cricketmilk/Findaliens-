Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Six — The Mews

He rode down to the valley on the quarter day, as he had ridden down on every quarter day since the founding, and Cinder came off the aerie ledge as his horse took the first switchback and did not follow him so much as accompany the mountain, riding its cold morning air in long unhurried reaches, above him and ahead of him the whole way down. The young hawks in the valley would know she was coming half an hour before any human eye found her. Work stopped in the mews when Cinder came down the sky. It was never ordered to. It stopped as talk stops in a guildhall when a founder's portrait walks in.

At the second switchback the mountain road kept its one crier post, a board and a bell under a slate hood, and the board wore the stark glyph that had replaced the old guild wreath on every post in the Concord these past years. The morning's proclamation was pasted fresh: the Founder announced that the age of the grey robes was ending, that engines would do what prayers had not, and that the worthy would live to see a new world. Beneath it, in smaller type, the tithe schedule that paid for his engine works stood unchanged. Aurelius read both. The Empire's one permitted rebel owned the wall his rebellion was posted on, and the tithes that built his engines, and, if the tower's oldest rumor ran true, a seat behind the screens he mocked. He filed it where he filed such things, and rode on down.

The valley opened below the last turn as it always did, and he let the horse stand a moment, because the years had not used up his need to look at it.

Stone the color of the mountain, built low and long. Eyries along the south wall, wolf runs under the birches, the salt pools steaming faintly by the springhouse. No bells. He had fought the halls over the bells in the founding year, fought them to the screens themselves, and won, and the mews kept its hours by light and by drum skin tapped twice, and a person could stand in the middle of the whole working valley and hear, under everything, water. Low doorways. Deep sills. Lamps shaded amber, corridors that curved instead of cornered, so that no one was ever surprised by a face arriving all at once. The inspectors from the halls walked through it every few years and wrote in their reports, approvingly, that the facility was austere. They had no word for what it was. It was a place built by a man who had spent forty years being hurt by rooms, for people who were hurt by rooms, and there was not another building like it under the Concord's sky, and the Chorus had paid for every stone of it and called it, in the ledgers, an asset housing.

The valley was only the half a visitor was shown. The other half went down: below the springhouse the undermews opened into the mountain's root, and it was there that the forming was done, in a dark total enough that no resonance the grid could send would ever reach to listen. A hawk was trained in the sky, because a hawk must fly. An austringer was made underground, because what was being made in him was the one thing the Empire could not be permitted to overhear, which was a mind learning to keep its own counsel.

The program's articles were his, written in the founding year and never once amended in the halls' favor. Enrollment by consent, and the consent renewed, aloud, every year at the quarter day, because a consent that cannot be withdrawn is a collar with better manners. No collar would ever close on a bonded throat; the corps flew uncollared and cognitively sovereign, or it did not fly. And the bond itself was the clinic's art turned all the way toward joy: attunement made not in any chamber but in a game, because a child and a fledgling learning to share a sky do it as children learn everything worth learning, by playing, and the deep courts of the undermews rang with more laughter than any facility in the Empire had a category for. His people were the best fed and best defended souls in the Concord, and the halls permitted it because the halls read the ledger as assets under maintenance, and he let the halls read whatever kept the articles standing. The taskings came down from the Chorus, and the corps flew them, when it flew them, on his signature and no other. The Empire had bought the program's product without ever once acquiring its loyalty, which ran, all of it, every wing, to one man, and if the halls ever fully understood that the valley was not theirs, they declined to write it down. It was the one thing he had built that was entirely what it appeared to be, and some evenings, at the court's edge, in the weather of his people, he found the thing the rest of his life had no line for, which was peace.

The corps flew more than its beasts. The valley's workshops built the made wings, constructed fliers of silverthread and hollow bone and a whisper of the bond art, half instrument and half creature, and the articles governed them too, in the one clause the halls' engineers found genuinely eccentric: a made wing could hold a heading, carry a burden, circle a fix, return, the light work, alone; but nothing made would ever act alone in any matter that could cost a life. Precision was a hand's work. An austringer rode the channel for anything of weight, present, governing, answerable, and when the engineers asked why the corps hobbled its own instruments, he gave them the finding in the cold form and watched it fail to land: the wing is not the weapon. The wing is the reach of a person. Remove the person, and you have not freed the instrument. You have orphaned it.

Both things were true. He had stopped being able to hold only one of them this quarter, and knowing why did not help.

The intake came first. It always came first; he had ordered the day that way years ago, on the theory that he owed the beginning of the thing his best hours, before the rest of the valley spent them.

Nine children this quarter. It had been four a quarter in the founding years. The registrar of intake, a patient woman named Edda who had been a handler until her bird took a border arrow, gave him the files in the amber lamplight, and he read them at once, entirely.

Nine children, eight, ten, and twelve years old, from five provinces, and the files all said the same thing, because they were built from the same instrument. Flagged by pattern of household resonance. That was the sentence now; in his day it had been men with ledgers walking the market towns, asking schoolmasters for the strange ones. And the instrument's idea of strange had widened, year on year, some threshold lowered above him without a paper ever saying so, as if the appetite had outgrown the crop. A household's stones sang its daily weather up the grid, and somewhere in the tower's cold floors an array combed the song for the signature of a child who did not tune, and the Empire that could not see its own sky lying had gotten very good, very quietly, at finding its doubled children before their own parents named them. The files even scored them. He had refused, for six years, to let the scores into the valley's records, and had lost, and the compromise was that the scores rode in a sealed sleeve at the back that the mews staff did not open. Edda had never once opened one. He knew, because he checked.

He met the nine of them in the long room with the east light, no table between, low stools, as it was always done. Parents were housed and fed up the valley for a fortnight, those who came. Seven had come. Two of the seven could not look at him, and made, when he passed, the low left hand sign he had been collecting since a moor road forty years ago; and the same two, at the fortnight's end, would press his hands at the gate and weep, and could not have said which of the two postures they meant. He had that effect at both ends of it, and had stopped trying to choose which half he had earned. Two children had arrived with escorts and hand receipts, and he read the receipts and filed, in the space he kept for such filings, the names of the chapter houses that had signed them out like freight.

The children were the children. Thirteen years of intakes, and the first hour never changed: the room full of held weather, nine separate storms of attention, each one bracing for this to be the next room in the long sequence of rooms that had already decided what they were. A boy from Vessen who rocked slightly, keeping tune with something honest. Twins from the coast who spoke to each other in finger shapes below their sleeves, a language of two. And at the end of the row, a fen girl called Wren, eight years old, who had not spoken in a year, since the assessors came for her sister's fitting and stayed to watch her instead, and who was watching, now, not his face like the others. His hands.

He was old at this. He did not speak to her either. He took the copper wire from his pocket, the working length, the one worn bright, and let it move through his fingers the way it moved when he thought, over, under, coil and release, and the girl's eyes followed it with the whole of her, the way his eyes had followed a basin's ripple as a boy, and after a while, without a word, he cut a fresh length from the spool in his case and laid it on the stool beside her, and moved on to the twins.

When he looked back, at the hour's end, her fingers were working it. Over, under. Coil and release. Not copying him. Improving on him, already; her left hand did a thing his had never learned.

"You are not broken," he told the nine of them, at the end, the sentence he had said to every intake since the first, the sentence he had built the valley to be able to say. "You are built for a different weather. The weather here is yours."

It was true. He had made it true, stone by stone, and he watched it land in nine faces with the force that truth lands when it arrives ten rooms too late, and underneath his own words, this quarter, for the first time, ran the annex he could not unread: per the archive's holdings. The grid had found these children. The grid found all of them now, faster and younger every year, the machine breeding its own antibodies and delivering them, in its perfect bookkeeping, to him. To the one man in the Empire who could receive them into shelter, and stood accused, in a hundred pages behind a blood lock, of being the reason they would need one.

Supper was taken all together, at the long tables, intake night or any night, because the founding articles had one clause he had fought for harder than the bells: no high table. He ate among the smallest, as the rule required of everyone including its author, and watched the valley do the thing no report had ever found a category for. The coast twins sat rigid through the soup, signing small and low under the table's edge, their language of two held close the way you hold the last of anything, and halfway through the bread an older girl across from them set down her spoon and answered in it. Hands up, open, unhurried: the valley's second tongue, taught in the first year to every child and half the staff, because a doubled mind is often a quiet one and the mews had long ago decided to meet its people where they lived. The twins sat very still. Then they began, both at once, at full speed, to talk with their hands to everyone in reach, a dam giving way, and the table answered them in kind, and their supper went cold, and nobody made them eat it. Further down, the boy from Vessen rocked his small tune against the bench, and the old handler beside him, without looking up from his plate, began tapping the wood at the boy's own rhythm, not correcting it, keeping it, the way you keep time with a colleague, and the boy's rocking eased into the shared beat and his shoulders came down from around his ears one finger's width at a time. At the meal's end came the mews' one fixed ceremony, which was not a sweet and was not a speech. The scrap pails went to the newest. That was the whole of it: the bones and the trimmings down to the wolf runs at dusk, carried by the nine, each walked out by whoever had carried the pail last intake, because the valley's way of saying you belong had never been a treat or a word. It was a duty with an animal on the end of it, and a child who has been a problem in every room of their life will carry a pail of bones past grown men with the bearing of a standard bearer, and the wolves, who had eaten at dusk their whole lives, sat their line at the runs like a receiving rank and made nine strangers into staff. Somewhere down the table, waiting for the pails, a child laughed at full voice, unguarded, at nothing, at bread thrown and caught, and not one of the nine flinched. That was the true intake examination, though it appeared in no protocol he had ever filed: the first laugh you do not brace against. Wren did not laugh. But the wire was going in one hand while the other held her pail in a grip that dared the Empire to reclaim it, and her eyes, following the thrown bread, held the beginning of the thing the valley was actually for, which was not safety, and was not shelter, and had never once been named in an appropriations hearing: the suspicion, arriving in a small fierce heart against all prior evidence, that a room might be glad you were in it.

He thought nothing at all for one hour, the valley's oldest gift to him, and the reason he had built it, whatever the papers behind the blood lock said.

The taskings came up with the courier rider at midday, under the quartermaster's seal, and he read the quarter's docket at Edda's desk as he read the halls' every communication with the valley: as a map of appetite.

The border eyes came first, the founding trade: two wings on the fords, one on the Saltmoot approaches, crossing counts, weather for the legions. That was the program the charter described, and it was the smallest part of the docket now. Below it, in the dry itemized grammar of requests that had stopped pretending to be requests, ran the other appetite. A wing over the fen districts, low passes at dusk, resonance survey, ahead of the season's regularized observance: his birds lent as pointers for the sweeps, the doubled sense that had been built to find lost children retasked to finding lit hearths. An escort flight for barges that carried no manifest he was cleared to read, billed to a house and not a hall, gilt buying wings as gilt bought everything now. And a request, third year running, from the office of ceremonies: flights over the provincial assemblies at the hour of the vote, high station, visible, for the dignity of the occasion, which meant: let the delegates vote under a hawk.

Then the folklore work. That was his private name for it; the docket said atmospheric reconnaissance; and the mechanics were nothing, a wing flown low and slow over a dissonant village at dusk, once, twice, silhouettes against the last light, no contact, no report. The report was filed by the village itself, in the only register it had left, which was talk, and within the month the criers' rural sheets would carry, in the corner kept for such things, accounts of lights, of visitations, of the Veil thinning and the old signs returning, the halls' own whisper mills dressing the halls' own instruments in the moor's oldest clothes, so that any soul who looked up and caught the Empire watching would report, instead, a spirit. Fear, delivered as folklore. Surveillance, explained as the otherworld, by the surveillers, at scale, on a schedule. He signed the flights he could not refuse and shaved the ones he could, and filed, in the pocket where he kept the age's ironies, the one he could tell nobody living: that the single being in the Concord who had actually fallen out of the sky spent his quarter days countersigning counterfeit visitations, so that a watched people would mistake the leash for the beyond. The Chorus manufactured the otherworld nightly, on a docket. The real one filed its taskings, and kept its own counsel.

Between the folklore work and the last item stood, this quarter, a novelty: a request over the Founder's own glyph, wanting a demonstration for the coastal works, a bonded pair arrayed in public while the handler stood waking, so that crowds could watch a man see through his bird's eyes. The charter forbade resonance apparatus on active bonded personnel, flatly, in drafting Aurelius had written himself, and stapled behind the request, prepared before the objection could be raised, rode the answer to it: an opinion from the office of the Remembrancer, three paragraphs in a hand so level it might have been printed, citing the precedent of the Third Siege, under which a serving austringer could be reclassified, for the duration of a demonstration, as architectural material, with liability for the silverthread assigned, in the event of the subject's death, to the subject's own estate. He read it twice, once for the content and once for the craft, and wrote DECLINED across it, and understood two things: that somewhere below the hall floors a man he had never met could make anything lawful, and that the word declined had begun, somewhere in that same office, to accrue a file.

Last on the docket, annual as winter, stood the sealed annex, and he refused it, as he had every year. The handlerless line, which the drafting had lately taken to calling, in the cantus's own liturgical grammar, the Seraph line. The engineering was newer each time; the sigil in the corner never changed, a stone with an open eye; and the ask under the drafting never changed either: his own made wings with the one eccentric clause deleted, the reach without the person, wings that would take tasking as arrays took script, with no austringer between the order and the talons to tire, or grieve, or refuse. He wrote DECLINED, PROGRAM FOUNDER across the seal in his own hand, thirteenth year running, and did not write what he had come to understand about the refusals: that they were being collected somewhere, like everything else, and that the machine that wanted his birds without their people was the same machine that wanted an Empire without its selves, and that his signature was one man's hand flat against a door with a century leaning on the far side of it.

The ward was on the north side, past the springhouse, where the water noise was best.

The Chorus paid for the ward under maintenance of assets. He had never told the residents what the budget line was called, and never would, and made himself reread the line each year at appropriations, as a discipline, the way the moor folk had once made children look at the winter sky to learn their size in it.

Sixteen residents this year. Handlers of the first and second wings, the ones who had flown when the bond was new and the doses were his earliest and the graying was still a thing he was promising them, in writing, in the enrollment articles, would be managed. Their names were on the honor boards in the refectory, polished weekly. The distance between the boards and the ward was four hundred paces. He had walked it so many times he could have told you where every board in the floor spoke.

Old Sarn sat where she always sat, in the east window, in the good chair, a woman of sixty whose wing had held the fords for a decade without one crossing lost, and who now kept a watch no roster held. She heard his step in the doorway and did not turn.

"Report," she said.

"Two crossings turned at the fords this month," he said, because you did not bring the ward lies, that was the founding rule of it, you brought them the true things shaped to the size of the room. "No blood. The grass is early."

"The grass is always early now." Sarn's eyes stayed east, on the mountains, or on the grass beyond the mountains that no window in the Empire could see. "You can taste it come up through the thermals. Green under the cold." Her hands lay in her lap, and she looked down at them presently, with a mild, patient puzzlement he had watched arrive in her over four years like weather crossing a plain. "These want looking at," she said. "Whose are these?"

"Yours, Sarn."

"Mine." She turned them over, considering, fair enough, a tribunal weighing testimony. "They're good hands," she allowed, finally, and let them rest, the question filed, not answered, and her eyes went east again, and he sat with her for the quarter hour the day owed her, in the water noise, in the amber light, beside a woman who had given the bond her selvage and her seam, and who tasted altitude in a shut room, and who had never once, in four years of the question, been told whose hands they were by anyone who was not in the room to hear the answer. The ward staff had standing instructions. The answer was always given. That was the whole of the medicine he had left to offer, and he knew, better than any man alive, exactly what it did not do.

Sarat's letter found him there, brought down the valley by the courier rider, and he read it by Sarn's window, which was either the worst place in the Empire to read it or the only honest one.

I respectfully decline the season, Sarat wrote, in the hand that swelled and shrank. The fords do not stand down and I am the fords' eyes. You built us to see. Let me see the winter through, and I will take the spring quiet, on my word. And then the date, written out formally, correctly, in a steady line, except that the year she had written was the founding year itself, the year of her enrollment, the year she was nineteen and the bond was new and he had signed the articles that promised her it would be managed.

He sat with the letter a long time. Sarn's window held the east. Somewhere out under it, on the winter grass, a woman was flying the best watch on the border with a hand that was writing its way backward through her own life, and her refusal was in front of him, and his order stood in the log, and both were true, and both were his.

He would let her fly. He knew it even there, in the ward, with the cost of letting people fly seated all around him in good chairs. The fords needed her, and she needed the fords, and the arithmetic of the thing had been fixed at the founding by the man who wrote the articles. All his mercies came due like this now, he thought. One at a time, by courier, in a failing hand, addressed to him personally.

He ended in the training court, at the day's last light, because the day was ordered that way too, and because he was owed one hour of the thing itself.

The middle wing was flying. Six birds up in the cold gold air, six handlers below in the open court, standing in the loose, tilted, listening stillness of the doubled, each one half here and half aloft, and the birds came down the valley in a long line abreast with the sun behind them, and turned as one thing, and the handlers below turned their faces with them as one thing, and for a moment the court was a single creature with twelve eyes, and it was, as it had been every quarter of the program's life, the most beautiful working in the Empire.

Cinder watched from the high wall, apart, as she was apart from everything, and the flying birds shaped their lines around her presence without ever nearing her.

He stood at the court's edge and counted.

Forty active handlers. Nine intakes this quarter, and rising every year. Eleven in the ward, and rising. One boy four floors under the archive, cleaning glass that was already clean. One bird on a wall that no instrument had ever read. And one man at a court's edge whom the glamour could not write an ending for, doing what he had done in a byre loft under the wrong stars, which was arithmetic, private, owed to no one.

The Chorus had gathered, into one valley and one program and one budget line, every mind in the Empire that its great instrument could not read, and had trained them to move as one creature, and had given them to each other, and to him. It had done this deliberately, carefully, over thirteen years, and had never once, in any annex of any paper behind any lock, priced what it had assembled. He had read a hundred pages of provisioning. The mews appeared in them twice, both times as a source of supply.

He did not write any names. It was not yet a list. It was only a count, standing in a cold court at last light, watching the one thing he had built that had never been turned into anything else.

Not yet, said the papers, in their bloodless grammar, from four floors under his ribs.
