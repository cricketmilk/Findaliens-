Part Two — The Breaking
Chapter Thirteen — The Correction

The thirty nine stones went back to their drawers on schedule, reseated, logged, correct to the dram. The fortieth went back six days late, out of sequence, posted to the return log in his own hand on the morning after he rode in from the west with snow still in his coat seams, and he entered it knowing exactly what it was: a flaw in a seam, small, single, the kind of thing no auditor would find because no auditor would look, and the kind of thing that did not need an auditor. It needed one person who counted things without being told to. He knew precisely one such person, and he had assigned that person to the fourth floor himself, and for eleven days after the western ride the boy said nothing, and cleaned glass, and was where the diagrams put him, and Aurelius began to permit himself the theory that a tired man had, for once, overestimated a risk.

On the twelfth day he came up from a session to find Aldric at the third bench, unasked, tending the slow distillation Aurelius had left running there, and the boy did not look up from the water bath, and said, in the flat procedural register the dormitories had issued him:

"The return log for the winter warrant carried an error. Forty drawn under the service warrant. Thirty nine returned on schedule. The fortieth posted six days late, out of sequence, against audit practice." A beat, exactly the length of a decision already made some days before. "I corrected it. It reads clean now."

Aurelius stood very still in his own doorway.

The distillation ticked over in its bath. It was the old preparation, the clinic's gentling, the first batch he had run since the clinic, assembled quietly from stores over a fortnight for the tapers still to come, and it was in no register anywhere in the tower, and the boy had been tending it for some hours by the look of the bath, correctly, and between them on the floor lay the plain arithmetic of what had just been said: that an acolyte of the fourth floor had discovered a falsification by a senior, and had not reported it, and had instead extended it, in his own hand, into the record, where it could never now be separated from him. The boy had not brought him a suspicion. He had brought him a completed crime, laid out on the bench like cleaned glass, and was standing beside it, waiting to find out what he had bought.

"Why," Aurelius said.

Aldric looked up then. Bronze eyes, dormitory posture, his hands resting flat on the bench in the way the dormitories teach children to park hands that want to shake, and when the answer came it came the way his true things always came, blunt, unpolished, four sentences assembled somewhere over eleven silent days and carried in whole.

"Because you went west with a case and came back with the same case and a stone that had traveled. Because you stood a woman down from the border for her own sake in the autumn, I filed the letter. Because this," a small movement of his chin at the bath, at the slow drip of the condensate, "is in no register, and it doesn't smell like the chamber." He paused, and something crossed his face that was not quite there and then was. "It smells like the cutting week. At home. Whatever it is you have started doing, Magister, it is the opposite of what we do downstairs."

The condensate fell, drop by drop, sweet and herbal in the cold air of the floor. Beebalm, among the rest. The boy had answered the question with his nose before any part of the tower could have answered it with an instrument, had sorted the whole of alchemy into two houses by the smell of his own coast at midwinter, and was standing in the correct house, waiting.

"I held the chalice," Aldric said. "I activated the array. My hands did that." He looked down at them, flat on the bench, steady as levels. "They should get to do the other thing too."

And then, because he was who he was, he closed it in procedure, with the words that were armor and oath in the same cloth:

"What else needs correcting?"

Aurelius crossed the floor and turned the bath's lamp down a quarter, because it wanted turning down, and because his hands needed a task while the rest of him caught up to the size of what was standing at his bench.

He had known this hour was coming since the morning after the screens. He had drafted, on various nights, various versions of what he would say in it, and standing in the actual hour he discarded all of them, because every draft had been built on a decision he now found he had no right to make alone, which was the decision of how much truth a person could carry. Ignis had made that decision for him for fourteen years, dose by loving dose. The vessel language sat in him still from the monthly dinner: Ignis on the subject of acolytes, expansive, pouring, a boy at that age is all vessel, what fills him now will be what he is, and then the Visionary had stopped, mid sentence, mid pour, for the first time at that table, and had looked into the cup he was filling for four full seconds, as if the sentence had turned in his hand on its way out, and had not finished it, and had set the pitcher down and spoken of the vigils instead. Aurelius had filed the stop and not understood it. He understood it now, standing where Ignis had stood, above an open vessel, with a pitcher in his hand, and the only honest difference available to him was the one he now chose, deliberately, and would keep choosing:

He would not fill the boy. He would show him true things, one at a time, at the pace the boy chose, and let what grew, grow.

"Sit down," Aurelius said. "There are rules. You will hear them before anything else, and if any one of them is unacceptable, you will finish the quarter on this floor, transfer with my commendation, and be troubled by none of this again. That door stands open at every hour of every day for as long as this lasts. That is the first rule and the others depend on it."

The boy sat.

"Second rule. You never again correct anything on my account without telling me first. Not from mistrust. Because two hands in one ledger, uncoordinated, is how honest men hang each other. If we falsify, we falsify together, knowingly, or not at all."

"Understood."

"Third. You will know what your hands need to know, and no more, and it will chafe, and you will hate me for it some mornings, and it stays. Not because you would break. Because what you do not hold cannot be taken from you in a chamber, and there are people in this whom I will protect from your courage as fiercely as from your failure. You will be one of those people to someone else in time. You will learn to hate yourself for it instead. That is the work."

The boy took that one more slowly. His eyes went, once, to the door, not looking for escape; measuring, Aurelius thought, the size of the thing he was inside now, the way he had measured the drawers, four aisles in. "Understood," he said again, quieter.

"Last. The truth about the stakes, once, tonight, in plain terms, because you are owed it before your hands touch anything else." Aurelius sat down across from him, bench between them, the gentling ticking through its bath at their side like a small patient clock. "What I have started cannot be finished. If it goes well it will still, very probably, end in the chamber downstairs, for me, and if I am careless or unlucky, for you, and the protocols we would face are ones I wrote. I am not building toward a victory. I am building toward a failure worth more than the alternative. If you want purpose that pays out, the Chorus is upstairs and it is hiring, and it genuinely rewards service, and I will say nothing. What is on offer at this bench is work that matters, odds that do not, and no absolution anywhere in it. For either of us."

Silence on the fourth floor. The condensate fell. Somewhere below them the tower hummed its held breath, forty floors of it, drawers and screens and dockets, the whole patient machine inside whose walls the boy had been raised the way a nail is raised inside a tree.

"The dormitories taught us that odds are weather," Aldric said at last. "You dress for it. You go out in it anyway. There was never anywhere to be but out in it." He looked at the bath, and then at his hands, and then, directly, at Aurelius, and finished in the register underneath the register, the one that came out blunt and landed harder than he meant: "You are the first thing I have ever been offered that wasn't already inside the walls. I am not going to transfer. Tell me what my hands need to know."

He started him small.

The task was a list. The chapter houses circulated returns registries each quarter, subjects restored to their districts, names, villages, dates, the attuned going home in the Empire's gentle bookkeeping, and the circulars crossed the fourth floor's correspondence desk routinely, read by no one, filed by pattern. The boy would read them. Nothing more. Read, remember, and build, in his head and nowhere else, the list: who had been sent back, and where, and when, within three days' ride of the tower. He was not to copy. He was not to annotate. He was not to hurry.

"A list of the returned," Aldric said. He was already, visibly, hurrying, somewhere behind the bronze eyes; the relief of belonging was in him like drink; and Aurelius watched the canon weakness surface exactly on schedule, the eagerness of a person handed a purpose after a lifetime of a functionary's blankness, and named it to himself, and began, that same hour, the long quiet work of being its counterweight.

"At the correspondence desk's pace," Aurelius said. "Which is glacial. You are a bored acolyte doing a tedious filing task badly assigned to you. Boredom is the costume. Wear it."

"Understood." A pause. "What is the list for?"

"Rule three."

The boy absorbed it, hated it, kept it. And in the days that followed, the circulars crossed the desk, and the glass got cleaned, and the gentling came off its seventh day sweet and stable and went into unmarked vials in a case that traveled sometimes to the west, and the tower noticed none of it, and the reason the tower noticed none of it, Aurelius understood now, watching the boy work, was the deepest irony in the Chorus's whole deep house.

They had built him. Fourteen years of dormitories had sanded the wrist out of his hand, the province out of his voice, the visibility out of his person. They had raised a child to be furniture, to be process, to pass through rooms and registers leaving nothing to catch a nail on, and they had succeeded to specification, and the result stood now at a correspondence desk on the fourth floor, unrecorded by any threat model in the halls, reading the registry of the Empire's emptied with a memory no instrument audited, in the service of the exact repair the whole apparatus existed to prevent.

The tower had spent fourteen years teaching the boy to be invisible to it. It had manufactured its own blind spot, polished it, certified it, and installed it on the fourth floor.

And the blind spot had chosen a side.
