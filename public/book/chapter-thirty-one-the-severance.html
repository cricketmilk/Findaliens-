Part Three — The Rending
Chapter Thirty One — The Severance

The arrays began to die before dawn, and they died the way lamps die in a house where no one is refilling them: not together, not dramatically, one hilltop at a time, each iron throat burning through the last of its reservoir and going dark, and the great roar of the grid, which had run under the Empire's bedrock for three nights like a tide that had forgotten how to turn, thinned, and faltered, and began, province by province, to go out.

Aurelius felt each station fail from the crown of the tower, the pressure behind the teeth easing in steps, and he should have felt only the relief of it, and did not, because he knew what the collapse of a resonance that size would drag with it. Everything the Chorus's art had ever paired was strung on that tide. The basins. The ledger webs. The collar net, already half abandoned, throats bared across the Concord where people had unbuckled the brass to hear the singing better. And the bonds.

The whipsaw came through in the grey of morning, when the great loom under the southern ranges let go of its mile of thought and the whole strung instrument of the Empire snapped back at once.

He heard it reach the aerie. Every young hawk in the high mews screamed in the same second, one voice out of forty throats, and he was already running for the stair when the second wave of it came through the stone itself, a shudder in the tower's silverthread like a nerve dying, and above the crown, riding the morning wind, Cinder took the whipsaw in open air.

She screamed once. It was not like the young hawks' scream; it had fury in it, the old familiar fury, her whole vocabulary; and then she banked hard on the crooked feather, the wind singing wrong across it the way it had sung for as long as he had known her, and she was through it, and she was unchanged, and she came down to the parapet rail with her feathers wrecked and her amber eye blazing and entirely, insolently, herself. The collapse could only take back what the Chorus had put in. The program's art had never governed her; she had spent her whole strange decorated life refusing to accept the bond's governance, and the refusal, which the rolls had entered as a defect, stood revealed this morning as the oldest sill in the Empire. There had never been anything of theirs in her to take.

The mews was not so lucky, and the accounting came by wing courier two days after, in Edda's handler hand, and he read it standing in the cold with the wind pulling at the pages, because some reports you do not sit down for.

Three hawks dead of the severance, all of the first breeding, their hearts stopping in the same grey hour on three different perches. Two handlers gone silent, not dead: the two who had stood nearest the southern loom when it let go, sitting now in the ward beside Sarn. One of them was the woman of the ninth page. She had asked, in her telling, that one sentence not be written down, and Edda had written it down, because a level hand keeps the record and not the comfort, and Aurelius read that sentence standing in the wind and made himself read it twice. Tal and her Burr alive and raging, the wolf bond younger and looser and both of them too stubborn for whiplash. The intake children shaken and whole; the bond had never yet been set in them; the collapse passed over the young like weather over seed still in the barn.

And Sarat.

Her wing had held the fords through all three nights of the ended world, riding the thermals over the panicking grass while empires broke behind her, and her bird, a broad old tiercel she had flown since the founding and called by no name anyone else was permitted to know, took the whipsaw at altitude in the dawn watch, and folded, and came down into the winter river, and did not come up. Sarat brought the wing home. The letter she sent after ran four lines, and Aurelius read it eight times before he understood what was wrong with it, and then read it once more and had to set it down.

The hand was level.

Two years of letters swelling and shrinking on the line, thermals for thresholds, dates written a generation astray, the graying working through her grip like frost through mortar, and now: four level lines, clerk straight, the hand she had signed her enrollment with. The bond had been the wound. Severed, the wound was gone, and the levelness of her hand was the emptiest thing he had ever read, because it had cost the other half of her senses and the last living thing that knew her private names, and she had written, in that terrible recovered steadiness: The fords are quiet. The grass will be early again. I find I can hold a pen correctly, and I would give it back. Tell me there was a reason, Magister. I will believe whichever answer comes in your own hand.

He wrote the answer the same hour, in his own hand, and it took him until dark, and it was six sentences long, and it was the hardest technical work of that whole winter, and none of it was a lie: there had been a reason; the reason had held; the fords she kept had kept a country; and the country, waking, did not yet know her name, and would, because he was the man who kept the ledger of kept names, and hers stood in it beside the tiercel's, which he did not know, and did not ask, because some names are owed their privacy even from the ledger.

Freedom purchased with trauma. He had written that phrase once, years ago, in a monograph on severance protocols, in the days when it was a technical projection. He spent that week learning what a sentence can be true and still be, again, from the far side.

The machine did not fall. It emptied.

That was the finding of the days that followed, and it surprised everyone but the fens, who had always known that the way you kill an institution is not to burn it but to stop believing it is load bearing. The screens convened once more, and what came out was not an edict but a silence that had lost its architecture: the anonymizing baffles cut, by whose order no one would ever establish, so that for the first time in the Chorus's history the seven voices came through as voices, separate, human, disagreeing, an old man's voice and a tired woman's voice and a frightened clever man's voice arguing in an open channel about custody of a corpse called the Concord, and the Empire listened to its hidden masters bicker for one full evening, and that was the end of the screens, because a mystery, heard plainly, is only a committee.

Vex resigned by broadcast, which was the only way he had ever done anything. It ran two minutes, went to every basin he still controlled, which was all of them, and consisted of a story about a resurrection act in the southern fairs, told properly this time, with the girl's name in it, and it ended with the sentence he had carried like a stone through a decade of velvet: We never stinted the corpse, friends. Tonight I am retiring the act. Mind who sells you your miracles; the good ones are free; I am told they always were. Then dead air, and then, because his architecture went on routing what the squares chose to sing, the fen songs, which needed no music director at all.

Word from the south came in pieces, and the pieces assembled into the age's plainest parable.

On the night of the eclipse, at the height of the working, the Founder's compound had fired its private Crossing, the engine built from the arrays' diverted iron, at the opening sky. The door had not opened. That was the whole of the event, and there were witnesses to every hour of it: the coast lit for miles, the great throat roaring at the seam of the world, and the seam declining. The Veil that night stood wide over every fire and threshold in the Concord. It answered songs. It answered names. It answered the called and the calling, and over the Founder's coast it stood like weather over a locked warehouse, because a threshold is an answer, and nothing in that compound had ever learned a word worth answering with. The engine burned through its stolen harvest and guttered. Behind the cloth, the far side moved on, unpurchased.

The manifest surfaced within the quiet days, printed by criers no thumb yet governed again. The berths: counted. The tiers: honored nowhere. The names: one household.

The crowds that had queued for passage did not burn the compound. The fens had taught the Concord a better instrument in those few bright days, and it was already working: they had simply stopped believing he was load bearing, and the glyph was coming down off the crier posts town by town, not by decree, by shrug, when the sky ended the argument for everyone. What the man who had owned the one channel became after that belongs to the histories of the scoured country, and they spend one line on him, which is one more than he ever spent on anyone.

From the east there came no parable, only roads. The army of the last verse had been provisioned for no winter, because there was to have been no winter, and the winter came as winters do. The legions of the Marshal Eventide, unpreached to in the new quiet, broke camp on the far bank of the fords and came home as men, on foot, in bands, trading spears for bread through provinces that no longer knelt.

Voss came to the fourth floor on the fourth morning, the last quiet one, unannounced as freight, and their sixth conversation was the shortest of the series, and for once she did not speak first. She set the black ledger on his bench, the cipher ledger, the one no other eyes had read in a working lifetime, and squared it to the edge, and stood there a moment with her docks built shoulders holding something his instruments could not weigh.

"Audit it," Voss said. "Someone honest should."

Two words of preamble she did not give, and none of apology, because apology was not in her syntax and both of them knew the ledger was the apology: every famine allowed, every shock unprevented, every liquidity crisis called like a note, entered in her own hand, at market rate, for the length of a career. The confession of the age, delivered as bookkeeping, to the one man in the Concord who read ledgers the way she did. He put his hand flat on the black cover and did not open it in front of her, which she took correctly, as the terms being accepted, and at the door she paused, back to him, the old habit.

"The famine province," she said. "In the year eight of the charter. That one was real, and I stopped it, and it cost nothing that mattered." A beat. "Enter that one first."

Ignis he found in the high chambers with the lamps unlit, sitting at the plain table in the winter daylight, and the daylight was not kind, and Ignis had forbidden it to be: the restoratives stood untouched on the sideboard, sent up by whichever loyal hand still sent such things, and the man at the table was greying by the day now, the harvest's arrears arriving in his face and hands like a tide honoring an old debt, and he was letting it, deliberately, with the same terrible thoroughness he had brought to everything, mortality performed as the only penance his inventory contained.

"I have been rereading the recensions," Ignis said, without greeting, an old man's voice now, and gestured at the table, where the Song's oldest settings lay open in facsimile, the equality error, the verses before the verses. "The first setting has no soloist anywhere in it. Did you know? All notes level before the Maker. We called it the equality error. We corrected it for forty years." His unaged hands, aging, turned a page with great care. "I have found no error, Aurelius. Only the correcting."

Whatever reckoning the world intended, it would never now get the chance to hold; and the one that mattered, the one at the plain table with the untouched restoratives, was already running, at its own pace, in the only court Ignis had ever truly answered to. Aurelius sat with him an hour in the cold light, and they spoke of distillation, and of olives, and of nothing at all that mattered, and it was the kindest hour he had it in him to give, and he gave it entire.

The tower, all that week, grew quiet floor by floor, the held breath letting itself out at last. And on the last evening, descending from the aerie, Aurelius stopped on the landing above the last brass door, because something in the deep stillness had changed, and he stood a long time in the dark of the stairs before his ear found it.

The hum below the ninth turning had stopped.

Whatever had run down there, under the bones, under the counting, for as long as the tower had stood on the mountain's threshold, it was not running now, and the silence that came up the stairwell in its place was not the silence of a dead machine. It was older and cleaner than that, and it moved like air moves when a long shut house is finally opened at both ends, and Cinder, who had refused those stairs her whole life, stood on the landing rail beside him looking down into the dark with her head cocked, considering, for the first time, the way down.

The machine had stopped. All of them had stopped. And underneath the stopped machines, patient as bones, the mountain had gone back to being a high place.
