Part Two — The Breaking
Chapter Twelve — The Taper

He drew forty stones on the winter service warrant, for reseating, rack by rack, in the plain sight of the day shift, and hers was the thirty first. The registrars checked the warrant against the audit schedule and found it in order, which it was; he had written the audit schedule. Forty housings went upstairs in a padded service crate to the fourth floor, where thirty nine of them would be reseated, logged, and returned within the week, correct to the dram, above suspicion.

The fortieth rode west in a field case, beside an ugly instrument of tin and green glass, on a horse, in the first hard snow of the winter.

His cover was his own protocol. The closure visit at thirty days had a follow at ninety, in districts of concern; he had drafted the clause himself, years ago, for completeness, as he drafted all such clauses, and no one had ever invoked it, and there was a special dry taste in invoking it now. Harrow Fell was a district of concern. The concern was his. The warden received the news of a second senior visit with an honor that had begun, Aurelius thought, to curdle very slightly at the edges into puzzlement, and would in time, if this went on, curdle further into a report. That was the arithmetic of covers: they were not walls, they were candles, and they burned at a known rate, and he worked by their light while he had it.

He dined with the warden. He praised the district's returns. He said the words routine and completeness in the flat register that made men stop listening, and at dusk he walked down the lane alone, as the protocol permitted, to the cottage at the west end, past the two bright horseshoes, one hung with mismatched nails, square and old beside round and new, two hands and some years apart in the fixing, past the sill where the milk jug stood catching the last light, and knocked, and was welcomed in by a woman with wax stained hands who did not know him, to the room where he was going to commit the second great crime of his life against her.

She made tea. That was the kind of thing she did now, easily, hospitably, a hostess in her one life, and he watched her move through the warm room with the lamplight on the racked candles, and he watched, in particular, her left hand.

He had seen it on the first visit and filed it without a name. At the shelf by the hearth, where a box of spills and tapers sat, her left hand, passing, had made a small motion: a reach, begun and retired, two inches of a gesture, like a word begun and swallowed. It came, he had established across that first evening, at thresholds of the day: at the lighting of the lamp, at the drawing of the curtain, at dusk. The hour of candles. Eleven years of a rite ran in that hand, below the reach of the anneal, below memory, in the place where the body banks what it refuses to surrender to the mind, and every evening at dusk the hand set out to keep the vigil and found nothing above it in the house of her self, no reason, no rite, no fells, and came back down, empty, and rested on the shelf as if it had only been steadying her.

The instruments would not have recorded it. He recorded it. It was the whole of his case, and he was aware, with a clarity he did not permit to soften, of what a thin case it was, and of the court he was arguing it before, which was himself.

"I have some further questions," he said. "For the record. It may take much of the evening."

"Of course." She set the tea before him. "There's no hurry here. The nights are long."

He asked her, then, the question he had promised himself he would ask, knowing what the answer would be worth, which was nothing, and knowing he would proceed on it anyway, and that the proceeding, not the asking, was the true shape of him now.

"If there were a treatment," he said, "that could return to you what the fever took. Weight. Grief. The reasons things mattered. It would hurt, possibly forever. Would you want it?"

She considered it with her mild, hostess's care, as if he had asked whether she wanted the window open.

"I'm told I nearly died," she said. "I feel very well now. It seems ungrateful to want to feel worse." And then, kindly, because his stillness must have looked like disappointment: "But you're the doctor from the tower. If you think it best."

If you think it best. There it was, the whole void where a woman's will had been, offered up to him with a smile, and he sat with his tea in the warm room and took the full weight of it: that consent could not be given by what the array had left, that the person who could refuse him had been dissolved by his own hand, and that he was therefore going to act on the authority of two inches of gesture in a left hand at dusk, and a request about fires recorded in a ledger line, and a drawer that beat first among four hundred. He had emptied her without her leave. He would fill her without it. The symmetry was exact, and it was damning, and he had turned it over for three hundred miles of snow, and the answer at the bottom of it had not changed: the alternative was to leave her in the peace he had manufactured, and call the leaving respect, and he had signed that form once already, twice, where the lines wanted, and he was not going to sign it again.

The gentling was in the second cup of tea. The old preparation, the clinic's own, mild as evening. He administered it in her house, by her hearth, without her knowledge, and did not look away from what that was.

The Mother stood on her table, cradle east, taper socket empty, and her stone sat in the cradle wearing its regulation chain, and the room, with the lamp turned down, held the three of them: the woman drowsing in her chair with her edges going soft, the machine that had once been a border surgery's whole hope, and the man.

He lit the taper from her own hearth. That was in no procedure; nothing tonight was in any procedure; but the Mother had been built in a room where Marrow lit everything from the banked fire because fuel cost money, and he found his hands doing it the old way, a spill from her embers, carried the two steps cupped, and the taper took, and the green glass came alive with it as it had first come alive under Marrow's lamp, and the resonance opened, slow, at the taper's pace, and the work began.

For three hours, nothing.

He kept the protocol he had drafted on the ride, the tone sequence, the graduated returns, her stone's impressions offered back through the Mother's throat at candle speed, the fells, the winters, her grandmother's voice, and the woman in the chair received them with her eyes closed and her breathing even and her face at rest, and nothing arrived. He watched the instruments he had; the instruments said the resonance was sound, the coupling clean, the offering made; and the offering lay where it fell, weightless, because a played memory is only a memory, and the Mother's true engine had never been the glass. He knew that. He had said it to nine intakes of acolytes in another life: the voice in the room does the work no instrument does. In the clinic the voice had been the patient's own, telling, paying the memory down. This patient had nothing to tell with. The paying had to come from somewhere, and the instruments had nowhere to get it, and the man in the room sat in the lamplight with three hours of nothing on his slate, his eyes raw from the taper's single point, and understood that the process was not failing. It was waiting. For him.

He set down the slate.

He took one of her candles from the rack, honey colored, dipped by hands that remembered below memory, and he set it on the sill of the west window and lit it from the taper, and drew back the curtain. Beyond the glass the snow had stopped, and the sky had opened the way the high country sky opens after snow, black and burning, and low in the west, at the fold of the fell, above her one flame, stood the star.

"There is a star in the west," Aurelius said, to the woman in the chair, in his own voice, not the session voice, not the register in which nothing important has ever been said, but the other one, the one that had come up through a gap in loft boards forty years ago with his breath showing. "Low, in the winter dark. The bright one. The catalogue calls it the Cantor's Lamp. That is what they printed." The taper burned. The stone in the cradle ticked, once, unhurried, on the eleven. "Its name is Glede. A banked coal. A fire that looks spent, and is not, and is carried through the dark to light the next one. You told me that. You corrected me, gently, without apology, the way a woman corrects a child's grip on a spoon. Not because my error was outrageous. Because the right way existed." His voice went on, level, paying. "And it was owed."

The candle stood in the window. The star stood over it. Eleven seconds went by, hill to hill, in the dark of the drawers three hundred miles east, and in the cradle her stone beat with them, and led.

And the woman in the chair, with her eyes still closed, in a voice with something under it, small, far down, but under it, said:

"Thessine."

Nothing else came. He waited, taper in hand, an hour more, and the word did not repeat and no second word followed it, and her breathing settled back toward sleep, and the something sank again below the surface of her face, slow, unhurried, like a coal settling into its bed of ash. But it sank the way a banked thing sinks. Not going out. Going down to wait.

She had said it the way you say a name at a door, when the house has stood empty a long time, and you knock, and you call the name into the dark hall, not certain, testing whether it still fits the rooms. Her own name, the first stone of her back in its socket, carried out of eleven years of dark in an iron box, and he stood in her warm room holding a taper with his hand shaking, a thing his hand had not done in the chamber, in the archive, before the screens, in all his years of the tower's worst floors, and he let it shake.

He put the room right before he left. Her stone back in its wrappings for the ride east; the Mother cased; the cups washed, because Marrow's first rule of the back room had been that you left no work for the grieving. The candle on the sill he did not touch. Let the village read it however the village read things. There was a fire in her window at last, one night of the winter, facing west, and it was wrong in every particular, lit by the wrong hand for the wrong reasons at the wrong hour, and it was burning.

At the hearth he stopped, and banked her fire for the morning, raking the coals together and seating the cover of ash over them the way he had watched crofters do it forty years ago, the way her hands would have done it, and he did it correctly, he found. He had been watching this country his whole life. Something in him had been taking notes for this the entire time.

She slept in her chair, veiled again, at peace again, and in the spring of her face, far under, a name lay banked where a nothing had been. The finding he entered on the ride home, with the snow starting again and the case heavy on the crupper, was two lines, and for once he read them back, into the dark, to the horse, to the hills, to whatever kept accounts out here where the tower's counting had never reached:

One word returned. It had cost a falsified warrant, a drugged cup, a stolen stone, and every remaining right he had to think of himself as her physician rather than her thief. Four hundred remained, and the arithmetic did not work, and could not be made to work, and he was going to do it anyway, one taper at a time, because the right way existed.

And it was owed.
