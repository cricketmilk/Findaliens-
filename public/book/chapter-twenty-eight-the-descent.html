Part Three — The Rending
Chapter Twenty Eight — The Descent

They collected him at dusk, four provosts and a hall prefect with a written order that bore, beneath Ignis's signature, the Remembrancer's level countersign, and it was done with a courtesy that told him everything about the optics the evening required: no shackles, no search, the senior alchemist of the fourth floor escorted up through his own tower in his good coat, past corridors lined with faces he had known his whole service, none of which knew yet what they were lining a corridor for. "No irons, Magister," the prefect said at the door, apologetic, holding the order flat on both palms like a tray being carried at a funeral. "The halls were particular on the point. You are to arrive as yourself." He said it as a kindness. It was the evening's whole design, spoken small, and neither of them knew it. The cantus wanted its deceiver arriving as a trusted man. The fall reads better from a height. He had written procedures himself for long enough to admire the craft, distantly, the way one admires the joinery of one's own scaffold.

The high stage stood on the crown terraces, built in a night and dressed in grey and whitewax: a platform under the open sky, the seven screens ranged behind it on the balcony, lit; the great projection arrays of the Needle's own station aimed upward all around it like a grove of iron throats; and above everything, the winter night, clear, hard, and wrong. The graining was visible now to any eye that looked, a weave in the dark coming and going at the edge of sight, and no one looked, because every eye on the terraces and every eye in the Concord was about to be given somewhere else to look.

The tower's thousands assembled. The basins opened, every channel, every square, every collar. Somewhere below, contested and unkilled, he could hear the other broadcast still running at the edges of the Chorus's own: squares singing their own songs in their own accents, ragged, unlicensed, alive. The night held both musics at once, and had not yet decided between them.

Ignis stood at the stage's edge in his plain grey, presiding, as the order of service required. He did not look at Aurelius at all. That was how Aurelius knew what it was costing, because Ignis had looked at him, warmly, easily, across every room they had ever shared, and had no practice at the alternative, and performed it like a man carrying furniture upstairs.

At full dark, the arrays fired, and the second act began.

He had read the stage directions, and the stage directions had not prepared him.

The light came down out of the zenith like a slow lightning, and inside it, descending, was a figure, and the figure was everyone. That was the glamour and the genius and the obscenity of it: a form woven at the intersection of forty prophecies and a hundred thousand harvested faces, tuned per collar, per district, per grief, so that every eye upturned across the Concord saw the descent of exactly what it had been promised, the Silenced Singer returning to the Empire's chapels, the Gatherer to the moors, the Ember Prophet to Osmara's shores, and to a man on the platform with pattern sight and no collar, nothing resolvable at all: a shimmer of stitched faces, a committee of light, beauty by aggregate, the Grisaille principle scaled to godhead, features that occurred elsewhere, all of them, everywhere, at once.

The voice was the same craft: every cadence, no source. It spoke, and the terraces fell to their knees in one grey wave, and it said what the cantus said it would say, mercy, arrival, the verse's end and the new verse's beginning, and on the stage below it two biers were carried forward bearing the two beloved witnesses, slain, the criers had wept for days, in the chaos of the first night, and the figure of light stretched out its committee of hands, and the dead sat up.

The sound the Concord made then came up through the stone of the tower like a tide. He felt it in his boots. Awe at planetary scale, awe as infrastructure, the crop of a hundred years harvested in one night, and Aurelius stood in his good coat between his courteous provosts, watching the resurrection act play to the largest bench in history, and heard, under the tide, with the ear the world had spent forty years training, the thin places in it. Squares where the roar was ragged. Channels where the singing did not stop for the miracle. A fen, somewhere, asking its risen darling for a whispered name.

Then the figure of light turned, in every square at once, toward the platform, and the second movement began, and it was him.

The voice read his life. It read it beautifully, in the cadence of scripture, each fact true and each weight false, the annex he had studied at his own bench come alive in the mouth of a committee of light: Aurelius Sol, a man from beyond the Veil, fallen among them in the guise of a healer. His name entire, pronounced complete before the world by the world's own voice, and he stood under the weight of the strangest of the night's many thefts: they had kept even that for him, all these years, filed and cold, to spend on this. Maker of the soul weapon. Corruptor of the Empire's Knights. Thief of the dead's rest, defiler of the archive, gatherer of fire cults against the Song at the hour of its fulfillment, and the arrays found him where he stood and lit him for every collar in the Concord, the Deceiver unveiled, and the provosts stripped the senior's braid from his good coat with ceremonial care, and the tide of awe curdled, on cue, at planetary scale, into hate.

He stood where they pointed. He did not fight it, because fighting it was in it. He did not flee it, because fleeing it was in it. He stood in the lit center of the world's hatred with a wire in his pocket and a taper in his coat, and made, of all the postures available to a man at his own unmasking, the one the cantus had no annotation for: he looked up, past the light, at the sky, and waited.

The figure of light raised its hands, and the Song drew breath.

He heard the third gallery arrays come live: a note under all the notes, the directed working Grisaille had named, spinning up its acquisition the way winter spins up a storm, and the voice of everyone announced, in every cadence, the sentence of the cantus: that the Song itself would now unmake the deceiver, breath meeting breath, that all might know the true from the false.

The breath of the Song came down the night like a blade of choir.

It swept the platform, and it did not find him.

He felt it pass, enormous and precise and blind, a hand groping across a table for a cup that was not there: the working hunting its mark by the one channel the Chorus had ever needed, the stone at the throat, the address every subject of the Concord carried against the skin, and finding, at the exact center of its stage, at the locus every array was pointed at, no address. No collar. No handle. Nothing to acquire. The controllers do not collar their own, and a woman with folded hands had seen to the rest before it ever mattered, and the breath of the Song, the executioner of the second act, the proof of the true against the false, stood on the stage of the world with its hand on an empty table, and swept again, and again found nothing, and the light around the figure of everyone flickered, once, with the smallest of stutters, the stutter of a performance arriving at a missed cue.

Silence spread up from the squares like frost. A hundred million knees, and no unmaking. The committee of light held its pose, hands raised, magnificent, and did nothing, because its next line required a corpse and the stage had declined to provide one, and somewhere behind the seven lit screens, he knew, the knife fight had gone from quiet to loud, and in the squares the other music, the ragged unlicensed living one, came up through the frost like grass.

Aurelius stood alone in the spotlight of the Concord, unmade by nothing, the Adversary unveiled and unslain, and above the crown of the tower, in the stunned and holding silence of the whole world, the grain of the sky stopped being deniable.

It opened along the zenith like a seam of light finding its own stitching, quietly, without thunder, and what showed through was not sky. It was pattern. Figure, pure and moving, the far side of every threshold the moor had ever tended, leaning through at last into the manufactured night, and the light of it fell on the terraces and the squares and the kneeling Concord with a weight no array had ever projected, and every soul under it, tuned or scattered, hollow or whole, felt the same thing at the same instant, which was: this one is not for us. This one is not aimed. This one is simply true.

Cinder's cry came down from above the opening sky, answered.

And in the held breath of the world, on the lit stage, in the space between a missed cue and whatever the universe intended to say next, Aurelius, whom no working could find, put his scarred hand into his coat, against the taper of eleven winters, and closed his eyes, and did the thing he had been refusing to do for fourteen years.

He replayed the last stone.
