Part Three — The Rending
Chapter Twenty Five — The Shadow

On the eve, the tower hummed like a struck bowl.

The crates were long gone to their provinces. The arrays stood ready on their hilltops, forty stills' worth of the anneal in their reservoirs, projector throats aimed at the sky. The wing had its instructions, one each, issued once: Sarat at the eastern fords, Tal in the western birches, the others at their stations along the chain. Ninety one doorsteps stood seated behind ninety one lintels. Forty hills held their wick bands, and beyond them hills he had never ridden, because the web had long since outgrown its founders, band by band, wife by carter's wife. A sewn book of ninety songs waited somewhere south with a voice that had a warrant to sing. And a boy who had taught his own hands their stillness cleaned glass on the fourth floor with a person who was not there folded and ready to wear.

None of it was enough, and he knew now exactly how much not enough, because he had come back from the northern fens six days before carrying a thing he had written into no plan, there being nothing to do with it but carry it. Everyone the night woke could be taken again. Everyone it could not reach would stay dark by their own choosing, having learned where the reaching led. The wing was ready, the doorsteps were seated, the songs were sewn, and none of it made a single house safe that a company of provosts could empty a second time before breakfast. He had spent the year proving that what was scattered could be gathered, and the last week of it learning that the gathered could be scattered again, and set harder the second time. He proceeded anyway. The alternative to a repair that might not hold was no repair at all, and that choice he had made a lifetime ago, on no ground, and remade every day since, and would remake tonight, carrying the woman in the fen cottage the way he carried the blanket in his case, folded and unused and unable to be set down.

Aurelius went up to the aerie in the last ordinary dusk of the world, and banked the brazier there correctly, ash over coal, and stood at the parapet with Cinder on her ledge, watching the sun go down wrong and beautiful over the Teeth, a sky the ledgers had spent twenty years tuning for tomorrow's performance, bleeding at the edges exactly on schedule.

Low in the west, early, the bright star stood at the fold of the world. Lamp. Glede. Milk Star. Watchfire. Latch.

"Eleven," he said to the bird, and to the Empire below, and to whatever kept accounts above the tuned and burning sky, and he counted it once, aloud, alone, the way the teaching required, and went down to sleep his three hours, and woke, in the black before dawn, on the last day of the verse.

The morning of the last day came up the color of old blood, and nobody in the tower remarked on it, because the sky had been rehearsing that color for a year, and because remarking had a schedule today like everything else.

The Needle assembled at the fourth hour. Vigil of the Final Cadence, the order of service said, every soul of the tower's service to the terraces and the great hall, robes of ceremony, collars of office, and Aurelius stood among the alchemical faculty on the high south terrace in the strange red morning with the wire in his pocket and a taper in his inner coat and watched the Chorus of the Lasting Concord gather, in its very best clothes, to watch the sky perform its lie.

Below the mountain, spread out under the wrongly colored light, the Empire waited. He could see the near town from the terrace, the chapter house square already full, the lanes already kneeling, and beyond it the whole visible west standing silent under the morning: no carts on the roads, no smoke from the workshops, a country holding its breath on instruction. The vigils had spent the better part of two years teaching them how to do this. Attend, the sermons had said. Kneel when the dark comes. Do not fear the rest between the verses. And the Empire, which had been taught for a century that obedience was harmony, knelt early and waited to be ended.

The basins opened at the fifth hour, every scrying pool and crier post in the Concord joining into one channel, and a voice came up out of them that every soul under the sky knew by heart.

Veyra sang the approach. The official settings, the approved verses, the Cadence liturgy she had toured for a year, and her voice rolled out over the terraces and the town and the kneeling roads, enormous, intimate, arriving in every square in the Empire at once, and Aurelius stood in the red light listening to the most heard voice in the world sing the Chorus's ending exactly as written, flawlessly, one last time, and only a man who had watched her count a claque could have heard what was under it: a singer pacing herself. A voice holding its true strength back, spending only what the script required, the way a boatwoman spends her arms on the out current, knowing what the return will cost.

Ignis stood at the terrace rail in his plain grey, lit like a bridegroom. Voss stood three ranks back with her ledger, watching freight. The screens had been carried out onto the high balcony, seven lit panels facing a dying sky, and somewhere behind them, or not, was a woman with folded hands, and Aurelius had stopped, finally, trying to know.

At the sixth hour, on the mark, to the minute his own tables had promised two winters ago, the limb of the moon touched the sun, and the last day of the verse began.

The dark came on the way the fear of God comes on: by degrees that could be argued with, and then past argument.

The light went strange first, thin and silver at the edges of things, shadows sharpening to knives, the red morning cooling to a color that had no name and no business over a living country. The birds stopped. The wind stopped. On the terraces the Chorus's thousands stood in their ceremony while the day was eaten in silence overhead, and in the town below, faintly, rising, he could hear the sound the sermons had promised would be singing and was not quite singing: ten thousand voices keeping tune against the dark, thin as ice, wanting to be a hymn and being, under it, only the oldest sound there was, the sound of people afraid together in a low light.

Totality came at the seventh hour, and the sun went out, and the stars came up at midday over the Concord, wrong and brilliant, the whole winter sky at noon, and for one breath the entire Empire, tower and town and kneeling roads, every soul under the shadow, was simply human together, silent, small, upturned, one animal under a dead sun.

Then the arrays fired, and the Rending began.

He felt it before any instrument could have shown it: the grid surging under the world like a tide going out through bedrock, every silverthread vein in the tower going taut, a pressure behind the teeth, behind the eyes, in the roots of the ears, and out across the darkened Empire, from forty hilltop stations at once, the greatest working in the history of his art went up into the manufactured night and came down through every collar in the Concord.

The town below him spoke with its dead.

That was what the annexes had meant, all along, in their bloodless grammar: narratives of conclusion, bespoke, per the archive's holdings, in the cadences of the harvested. The flood poured down through the collars, and to every throat it came wearing a voice that throat had loved: mothers dead twenty years singing the end gently to their kneeling sons; drowned brothers speaking from the dark to porters on their knees in the chapter square; a hundred thousand privately beloved dead, distilled out of the drawers' impressions and played back through the stones against the skin, each one saying, in the one voice its hearer could not disbelieve, the same engineered sentence: it is over, beloved. The verse is ending. Lay it down. And under the dead sun, wrapped in the voices of everything it had ever lost, the Empire began, lane by lane, square by square, to lay it down.

Three ranks along the terrace from him, a young registrar of the fourth floor, a girl who filed decay curves and had never once misfiled one, put her hand to the collar of office at her throat, and her face opened like a door. "Gran," she said, aloud, to no one the terrace could see, in the voice of a child at a kitchen threshold. "Gran, I kept the ribbons." And whatever the stone against her skin said back to her, in whatever kitchen it had been distilled from, she believed it, entirely, the way she had believed it at six, and she wept with a joy that was the purest thing on that terrace and the most stolen, and Aurelius stood in his robes of ceremony four paces away and could do nothing for her at all except what he did, which was to witness it, and enter her among the owed.

He watched it from the terrace and did not look away. Serenity moving through the kneeling town like frost across a pane, faces unclenching, weeping stopping mid sob, the terrible economy of it, grief resolved at last by the counterfeit of everything grief had ever wanted, and it worked, that was the whole black heart of the matter, for the tuned and the thinned and the worn down millions the Rending worked exactly as designed, and the design was mercy shaped, and the mercy was the harvest, and on the high terrace around him the Chorus watched its century arrive and wept with joy in the midday dark.

Not all of it wept. Along the rail from him a Voice of the second rank stood with a slate tilted to the starlight, reading uptake the way brokers read a closing price, and when the serenity took the near town whole, lane by lane, he saw the man's mouth move, and leaned a fraction to hear what a soul says at the emptying of a hundred thousand of its neighbors, and what the man said, softly, with satisfaction, to his slate, was: "And they told us the fens would be difficult." Three paces apart on one terrace: the rapture and the bookkeeping, the weeping and the tally, and Aurelius, filing both, understood that they were not two responses. They were the same religion, worshipping the same instrument, in its two authorized postures.

Ignis's face, lit by starlight at noon, was the face of a man seeing the kindness he had promised the world finally being done to it.

And then, in the west, on the shoulder of the farthest visible fell, small and orange against the false night: a fire.

Aurelius stood very still at the rail.

Eleven seconds. He counted them with the whole of his body, the old reflex made prayer, and on the eleventh, one shoulder further west, a second light answered. Eleven more. A third, at the edge of seeing. The chain walking the tops at its patient pace, hill to hill, out of the west, into the manufactured dark, tended fires speaking to tended fires down a line forty hills long and longer now, lit not against the eclipse, because the moor had never feared the sky, but against the flood, the wild even uncounted fire pouring down through every collar, and beside the beacons' answer the whole vast working of the Rending stood suddenly revealed, to any eye that knew the difference, as what it was. A hillside simply alight. A fire nobody tended. A voice with no name to give.

"The welcome fires," said a Voice of the second rank, further down the rail, moved almost to tears by the sight, reading the cantus's own caption off the hills. "Look. Even the far shepherds keep the vigil."

"Even them," Aurelius agreed, senior to junior, level as a certification, and counted the eleventh second with the whole of his body as the third fire answered.

Out there in the dark, in nine provinces, in warehouses and lanes, the manufactured dead were arriving at four hundred and six drawers and ninety one doorsteps with their beautiful borrowed voices, and finding the doors shut, and knocking, and having no name, and he could feel it, faintly, under the grid's great roar: the congregation holding. Stones singing their own eleven inside the flood. Houses answering their doors and asking, in the threshold's own grammar, who is it, and receiving no answer, because evenness cannot knock and a counterfeit cannot say its true name.

The flood had been built for a country with no thresholds. It was finding, in the dark, in its first hour, the thresholds nobody had modeled: shut doors in the drawers, iron on the lintels of ninety one lanes, fires on the high places keeping a count older than the Empire, and a sky full of midday stars in which, low in the west, at the fold of the world where the shadow's edge was already silvering toward return, the bright star stood over the beacon line like a name over its signatures.

The sun came back at the seventh hour's end, as his tables had always said it would. The dark did not.

That was the design's last cruelty and he had known it was coming: the sky's performance ended, and the Rending's went on, the collars still speaking, the dead still singing their gentle engineered ending into a hundred thousand kneeling ears under a return of ordinary daylight that no one below seemed able to see anymore. The Empire stayed on its knees in the light, listening to the dark.

On the terrace, the Chorus began the recessional hymn. Aurelius sang it with them, level, correct, a senior of the fourth floor in his robes of ceremony, and under the hymn, at the floor of himself, he kept the other count, the spoken thing, the eleven, hill to hill, and sang the Empire's ending in tune with it, and did not miss a beat of either.
