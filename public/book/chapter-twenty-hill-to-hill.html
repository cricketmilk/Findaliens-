Part Two — The Breaking
Chapter Twenty — Hill to Hill

The last ride west ran ahead of the freight schedule and behind the weather, and he made it knowing it was the last: the circuit's cover was burned to the socket, the wayposts had provosts now, and after the first of winter the man he was about to become, the returned son, the grateful colleague, would not be able to explain a district inspection to anyone. Whatever the west still had to give him, it had to give him now.

It gave him, first, on the second evening, a vigil.

The hill town was on the cover's route, and the chapter house was lit, and a district inspector who rode past a Cadence vigil unremarked would have been remarked, so he stood at the back wall among the carters and the late trade, in the smell of wet wool and cheap wax, and heard the most famous voice in the Empire fill a hall the size of a barn as evenly as it had filled the Needle's sanctum, because Veyra did not sing down to rooms, that was the whole of her legend in one fact, the districts loved her the way you love someone who has never once shown you a cheaper version of themselves. She sang the approved settings. He counted from the back wall, out of habit, and found the seeded threes still in the crowd, thinner out here, two voices and bored ones, the narrative sphere economizing on provinces it considered already bought.

Then, in the last verse, in the middle of the recension's smoothed and lawful line, she turned one note.

It was nothing. A grace on the refrain, a small wet turn the fens put on a held vowel, the ornament the archivists had sanded off every setting forty years ago, and she dropped it in without a flicker, one degree of true in a gallon of paint, and the young in the hall heard nothing at all, and every grey head in the room came up at once, the way hills answer. An old shepherd by the door stopped breathing for two bars. A grandmother in the third bench put her hand flat on the wood in front of her, the gesture of a woman steadying at a threshold, and Veyra, eyes down, exact, performer's humility, took her soundings and gave no sign, and Aurelius stood against the back wall with his blood loud in his ears and understood what he was watching. Not a performance. A survey. She was walking her warrant through the provinces one vigil at a time and counting, hall by hall, head by grey head, what still lived under the recension. What the borrowing had not managed to kill.

He did not approach her. The craft forbade it and the account under her luck watched her closer than any provost, and the second conversation of their acquaintance would have to keep. But he stayed at the wall while the hall emptied, longer than cover strictly allowed, because the hall did not empty entirely: the young went out into the cold talking of her dress, and the grandmothers stayed, the way, he would learn within the year, the grandmothers stayed everywhere, and the most heard voice in the Empire came down off the little platform, and sat on a bench among them in her plain cloak, and inclined her head, and listened.

"That was my mother's turn, on the refrain," he heard one of them say, an old woman's voice, half accusation, half prayer. "Sanded off before you were weaned, girl. Where did you have it?"

"Under the paint," Veyra said. "There is more under there. Sing me yours."

He rode out before he could be counted himself, north and west into the weather, and entered the evening's finding at the first waypost: the Chorus had bought the voice nine years ago, at a Corvara counting house, and thought the purchase final. Nobody had told them what he had known since a receiving line: that she counted everything. The voice was buying itself back, vigil by vigil, hymn by hymn, and had begun, quietly, taking inventory of the estate.

It gave him, after, a changed cottage. He felt it from the lane: the same warm windows, the same racked candles, and under it a texture his senses read before his mind did, as they read rooms, entirely. The cottage had traffic now. Not visitors; nothing so visible; traffic. A boot scrape by the gate too worn for one woman's comings. Wax orders pinned by the door in a column meant to be read at a glance from horseback. The house of a mild attuned chandler, doing a mild trade, and threaded through its dailiness, invisible to any census, the unmistakable grain of a working node.

"You've been organizing," he said.

"I have been selling candles." Thessine set the tea down, and her eyes had the vigil in them banked to coals, the public mildness she wore now like ash over a fire. "It happens that people order candles for the winter. It happens that the winter has fire nights in it, and that a chandler must discuss wicks, and bands, and burning times, with many households, across three districts. Commerce, Magister. The census loves commerce." She sat. "The wick bands carry the order of the lighting. A twist for the count, a color for the night. My grandmother's system; I did not invent it; I only remembered it. You will want to know how far it reaches now."

"How far."

"Forty hills," she said. "By midwinter, more."

She had known about the manifest before he said it. That stopped him for a moment, until she explained, and the explanation rearranged his whole autumn: a carter who hauled for the chapter houses had a wife who bought wicks; the wife had mentioned crating schedules; the crating schedules had moved up the fells, band by band, weeks ago. The Bearers' web had known the archive was shipping before the fourth floor had, because freight needs carters and carters have wives and the census has never once thought to collar a conversation about wicks.

"They are taking my years to a warehouse," Thessine said, in the tone of a woman confirming an order. "You mean to teach the stone its doorstep first. You mean to do it by going back into their warmth and being trusted." She read the plan off him before he had shaped the second sentence of it, and she sat with it, and did not tell him it was dangerous, because she did not spend words on the self evident. What she said instead was: "You will need wax."

She went to the racks and took down a taper, and he saw that it had been made already, made for this, waiting: honey colored, dipped smooth, banded at the base with a twist he did not know.

"My winters are in the wax. Eleven of them; the bees do not forget, whatever else was taken." She set it in his hands the way the moor handed things, directly, without ceremony that would have cheapened it. "Your sill wants grain, you told me. The stone's grain is me. When you stand at my drawer, burn this at it, and do the count aloud, and it will know its own." A pause, and then, in the correcting voice: "Aloud, Magister. Not in your head. The count is a spoken thing. It has been a spoken thing for longer than there have been empires to overhear it."

"Understood," he said, and meant it in every register he had.

"Good. Now finish your tea. We are climbing tonight, and you will want your legs."

The first fire night of winter came down on the fells clear and enormous, stars from ridge to ridge, and they climbed the western shoulder in the early dark, the two of them and Brann's boy ahead with the glede box, a serious child of eleven who carried the banked coal as acolytes carry relics, and who had been taught, this year, at last, by the person whose office it was to teach him.

There were others at the cairn. He had expected a fire; he had not expected a congregation: nine souls on the high ground in the wind, come up from three villages by paths that met nowhere the road could see, shepherds and a wheelwright and a midwife and the eldest of them, a shepherd woman called old Cade, small and knotted as thorn, who looked at Aurelius once, all the way through, in the way of the very old on the high hills, and said to Thessine, as if concluding a long conversation:

"So. You brought him."

They built the fire at the cairn's east face, out of the wind, and Brann's boy opened the glede box and fed the carried coal into the kindling with two hands, grave as a coronation, and the fire took, and when it stood, old Cade looked west along the darkening chain of the fells and began, without announcement, the rite.

It was not what his cataloguing years had filed it as. He had the folklorist's version: fires for luck, fires for the dead, a pretty stubbornness. What happened on the hill was work. The fire was fed to a steady height and held there, and the cold came into his face over the flames, the first fact of the world, still true; the boy counted, aloud, unhurried, the spoken eleven, and on the eleventh the whole company turned west, and waited, and out on the next shoulder of the dark, small as a held breath, a light answered. Eleven counted seconds; then the third hill, further; then a fourth, a spark at the edge of seeing; the beacon line walking the tops at its patient pace, hill to hill, tended fire speaking to tended fire down a chain the census had never mapped and the Chorus had never silenced, forty hills long tonight and lengthening, and Aurelius stood in the wind and watched the sentence being said slowly across the roof of the world and heard, under his own breastbone, where a boy had once watched from doorways that suffered him, the reaching thing go quiet.

"You'll be wondering what it is for," old Cade said, beside him, not looking at him. "The clever ones always think it's for the dead. The dead don't need finding, they're where we put them." She fed the fire a length of thorn. "It's for the scattered. Them that crossed wrong, coming into the world. Souls tear, crossing, if they catch, and a torn soul lives its whole life half here and half there, and on the dark nights the far half hears better. So we light the tops. So the lost half of them knows where the hearth side is. So none of them, not one, ever stands in the dark thinking nobody kept a light."

The fire cracked. The next hill west burned steady at its eleven.

"And once in a long age," the old woman said, into the wind, in the tone of a thing recited because it must be recited whether or not anyone present needs it, "one crosses whole. Falls right through, all of a piece, and can't tear back, because there's no torn edge on them to find the seam by. The whole ones are the worst lost of all. The tellings say you'll know them because they'll be strange in the world all their days, and count what nobody counts, and learn everything and belong to nothing." She reached out with her thorn stick and settled a log, precise as a chandler. "The fires were always mostly for them. That's the old teaching. My gran had it from hers: when you light the tops, light them as if somewhere down in the dark there's one who fell through whole, watching, and doesn't know yet that the light is his."

Aurelius stood very still on the roof of the world.

Forty years. Forty years of doorways and lofts, of the lights going up the winter dark one by one while whatever reached in him went quiet and he did not know why; forty years of filing the fire nights under folklore, under whispered knowledge, under useless things carried privately, his; forty years of being the strangest scholar of a world that he believed had never once addressed him, and the whole time, every winter, on every hill, in a code he had catalogued without ever once decoding, the world had been leaving a light on. For him. Specifically, in the oldest specification the moor possessed, for him: the one who fell through whole, watching from the dark, who didn't know the light was his.

He did not weep. His grief had never once in forty years come out by that door and it did not find the door now. What happened instead was quieter and larger: somewhere in the black ledger of his life a column he had carried since the cold grass, a column headed alone and totaled nightly for four decades, was audited, found to be in error, and closed.

Then the company joined hands at the fire, the midwife on his left, Thessine on his right, and said the creed the way the moor said everything, once, plainly, spending the words like lamp oil, and he had never heard it, in all his cataloguing he had never once heard it said aloud, and it came around the fire like the count itself, hill to hill:

"What is scattered may be gathered."

His own language. That was the fact of it, the fact he would carry down the fell and east into whatever the winter meant to do to him: pattern, loss, return, the whole grammar of his existence, compressed into six words by shepherds, and spoken on a hill, in the wind, in the one tongue he had been listening for since the wrong stars, and it had been here the entire time, banked under the Empire's floor, waiting for him to stand close enough to a tended fire to hear it.

Thessine walked him down as far as the lane. At the gate she stopped, and looked back up at the shoulder of the fell, where their fire stood steady, keeping its count, answered and answering.

"Four winters I kept the western tops," she said. "The charges were correct, whatever else was wrong in that room. Four winters, every dark night, up that path with the box." She was quiet a moment. "I used to wonder, some nights, what he looked like. The one who fell through whole. Whether he was even real, or just the teaching's way of making us climb in the rain. You wonder things, on a hill, alone." She turned to him, and the vigil eyes held him the way they had held him across a ledger a year ago, entirely, without apology. "Then a man from the tower read me my charges, and his voice was level, and his eyes tracked something to the left of me all that morning, as eyes do when a man is counting what nobody counts. I did not understand what I was looking at. I understand it now."

The wind came down the lane between them, smelling of snow and fire.

"I was lighting your fire, Magister," Thessine said. "Eleven years. Yours before ever I knew you, and yours after what you did to me, because the teaching does not have exceptions, that is what makes it a teaching. What is scattered may be gathered. Even the gatherer. Even you." And then, because she was who she was, she added, in the voice she trimmed wicks with, turning back toward her cottage and her candle burning in its window: "Ride east. Teach my stone its doorstep. And say the count aloud."

He rode east in the dark with a taper of eleven winters wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, down out of the high country where the hills went on quietly talking to each other in a language older than the roads, and the finding he entered that night had no cold form left in it anywhere, and he wrote it as it came:

He had spent forty years learning this world by heart, the way a man learns a language he was not born speaking. Tonight, on a hill, in firelight, the world had answered him in his.

It had known his name the whole time. It had been saying it, at eleven second intervals, from every high place in the west, every winter of his life. He had thought he was the only one keeping count. He had never once been the only one keeping count.
