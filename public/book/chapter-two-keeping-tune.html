Part One — The Hollowing
Chapter Two — Keeping Tune

He came down from the aerie at some black hour with the blanket folded back into its case, and did not stop at the dormitory landing. At the bottom of the stairs was the work. Sleep was a country that required an application, and he never quite filed one.

The laboratory floor at night belonged to him. Cold stands along the north wall. The projector chambers sealed, their brass wheels dogged down, holding their dark the way jars hold preserves. He lit two lamps, no more. The silverthread in the walls took what little he gave it and paid it back thin and brown, and under the light ran the thread's other payment, the one nobody on the floor had ever reported because nobody else could file it: a hum pitched just below the shelf where hearing keeps things, which he did not hear so much as wear, all day, every day, like grit in a boot, and had worn for thirty years, and would notice, he suspected, only on the day it stopped. In that light he unlocked the correspondence drawer and took out the week's packet from the mews.

No one else sorted this. In all his years on the floor no one had asked to. The Chorus did not care what passed between him and his austringers; it cared what his austringers could see, and that was totalled elsewhere, in the border forts, by men who were paid to count crossings and were not paid to count anything else.

He counted everything else.

The packet from the third fort was in Sarat's hand. Twelve years bonded, senior on the eastern grass, and her hand had been a clerk's hand once, small and level. It was not level now. The letters swelled and shrank along the line, the way handwriting does when the writer is listening to something else while she writes. Two crossings turned at the fords, she wrote. No blood. The grass is early. We rode the thermals over the herds at first light and nothing moves out there that we have not counted. And at the close, where the standard form asked how long her wing could hold its watch, she had written: through the thermals of winter, as ever.

He read it twice, not because it was unclear. Because it was.

Thermals, where thresholds should have stood. The same word, once meant and once escaped, and she had not caught the second one. This was a woman who had once turned her whole wing back from the fords on no evidence but a wrongness in the light over the grass, and been right, three days before the riders showed on any glass. Twelve years of that, and the first thing ever to get past her had not crossed the grass at all. It had left from inside. He sat still and let the letter lie in the lamplight, and there it was, the thing he never permitted himself to say in front of the young ones: the graying does not knock. It arrives as one wrong word on a level line. Later it arrives as the taste of altitude in a shut room; as eyes that let a tossed apple drop because half the senses that would have tracked it are aloft over grass three hundred miles away; as cold settling at the crown of the head in a warm room, the body misfiling which sky it stands under; as a woman looking at her own hands on a table and taking a moment, one beat too long, to claim them. He had watched it finish senior handlers whose names were painted on the honor boards in the mews refectory, and the boards did not say what it finished them into.

He had built the anneal for this. That was the true first use, whatever the registers said now. A softening for the bonded, so that the doubled senses would stop wearing the self away at the seam. It had helped. Not enough, and he had said so, in a report he could still recite, and one evening Ignis had come down to this floor and stood where the couriers stand, warm and unhurried, lending the spot the honor of his patience, and had asked his gentle question. Whether a preparation that could ease the edges of a soul might not, in principle, be persuaded to keep going. He had asked it in the voice reserved elsewhere for asking after a colleague's mother, and that was the lesson under the lesson, the one Aurelius took years to let himself learn: there was no floor under the warmth. It went all the way down, and at the bottom of it something was burning.

In principle. He had answered the alchemy honestly, because it had not occurred to him yet that honesty was a raw material like any other, and that the Needle wasted nothing.

He took a fresh sheet and wrote the order standing Sarat down from the watch for a season. She would refuse. The forts would support her refusal, because her wing was the best on the grass and the winter crossings were coming. The order would change nothing, and he wrote it anyway, dated it, entered it in the log, because one day someone would tally the true price of the birds, and when they did, there would be one line, in his hand, that had tried.

The copper wire went between his fingers awhile.

He did not think about the woman from Harrow Fell. He arranged his attention around her as the moor people were said to build their houses, around a hearth already standing: nothing looked at it, and everything faced it.

Aldric came onto the floor at the sixth hour, which was his hour, and stopped inside the door.

The lamps were already up. The senior alchemist was already at the bench. Neither of them named the night before: the sealed chamber, the chalice set down half a beat early, the one word that had escaped through the flaw in the boy's seam and could never be gathered back into it. A moth had got into the lamp hood overnight and died there, and its small shadow lay inside the light on the bench, and Aurelius worked around the dimness all morning rather than open the hood, for reasons he did not audit.

Theft. Spoken in a working chamber, during a sanctioned session, in the hearing of a senior of the fourth floor. Aurelius knew the paragraph that covered it. He had signed the ledger line that faced that paragraph more than once, for less, in years when he had still believed that the machine's rules and the world's rules were the same set. And the boy knew he knew. That was what stood in the doorway wearing an acolyte's face: a person who had passed the night deciding whether to run, and had come to his own bench at his own hour instead, to be destroyed, if he was going to be destroyed, standing where his work was.

Aurelius had meant to do nothing. Twenty years of doing nothing, correctly, at every crack in every wall, had made him what the Chorus was pleased to call reliable.

"The stands want cleaning," he said. "The seventh especially. There's a residue in the neck I don't like the color of."

The boy's eyes moved to the seventh stand and back.

"Quicksalt cloth. Not the flannel. The flannel sheds, and then in the spring we are distilling flannel and publishing the results."

It was not a joke. His humor never quite was; it was observation that happened to land crooked. But he watched some fraction of the night go out of the boy's shoulders, and Aldric crossed to the cabinet and took down the cloth, and the floor filled with the small honest sounds of a careful man cleaning glass. There is no lying in that sound. Aurelius worked at his bench and did not watch him, and let the morning stand itself up around the two of them, and waited. He did not push the door. He stood where he could be seen from the other side, and kept his hands still, and let the one inside choose the hour.

The hour came at the eighth, from the side, as such things come.

"The coast cut the terraces at midwinter." The boy did not look up from the glass. "In the south. Before the schools."

"Did it."

"One week. All of it, every shed drying at once, and the smell stood over the whole road, and everything turned on that week. The boats came in for it. The year came in for it." The cloth kept moving, small circles, the discipline holding the voice level. "I never learned the plant's name. It doesn't grow in the north, and the dormitories don't." He stopped there. The sentence had walked him further than he had meant to go, and he let it end where his nerve did.

Aurelius set down his pen.

"The herbals write it melissa serrata," he said. "Bee leaf, roughly, if you crack the Latin open. It grows in the tide cut terraces above the coast road, and nowhere else in the Empire, and the terrace people never called it anything the herbals write." He looked at the window rather than the boy; it was easier to hand a thing over without watching it be received. "Your coast would have said beebalm."

The cloth stopped.

"Beebalm," Aldric said.

He said it like a man saying a password he has carried for years without knowing which door it belonged to. One word, and behind it, audibly, a coast assembled itself: the sheds, the smell standing over the road, and a pair of hands he could no longer put a face above, small at the edge of a shed's heat, stripping stems faster than a boy's eyes could follow. The boy stood holding a piece of laboratory glass in a tower at the roof of the world, six years old for the length of a breath.

"They cut it all in one week at the ebb of the year," Aurelius said, "because cut later it bitters. So a whole year would pass without that smell, and then one morning it would stand over the coast again, shed to shed, and the year would fold shut like a ledger. That was the use of the week. It was not a harvest. It was a clock."

"The chapter house sells it dried now," the boy said. "At the Festival. In paper twists, with the glyph on. It's correct." A pause, the exact length of a decision. "It doesn't smell like anything."

"No," Aurelius said. "That's the recipe."

The sentence stayed where he had set it. He did not take it back. It was the least deniable thing he had said aloud in fourteen years, and the old cold part of him, the part that survived by counting exits, noted that there was now a witness to it, and the other part, the part that had come down from the aerie in the dark with a folded blanket and an unspent tenderness the size of a province, found that it did not care.

Aldric turned back to the seventh stand. He worked the neck of it a while, and then he said, in the flat register the dormitories had issued him along with the robes, "I'll finish the stands," and the words were procedure, and the procedure had four words too many in it, and every one of them meant yes.

Aurelius returned to his bench. His hands had begun doing something with the wire that he had not authorized. He considered stopping them, and did not.

The boy worked. At some point Aurelius stopped hearing the cloth and went where he went, the place his floor had long since learned not to knock on: eyes at the middle distance, wire turning, a man auditing a ledger nobody else could read. The journeymen had a dozen names for it and used none of them to his face. When he came back it was all at once and completely, the way a lamp is unshaded, and the first thing his attention landed on was always, disconcertingly, the exact thing its object was trying to hide.

He knew recruitment. He had crossed forty years of this country gathering the scattered out of alleys and sickrooms and the academies' triage rooms, and he could say in his sleep the sentence that unlocked a doubled child: you are not broken, you are only built for a different weather. This was not that. There was no program behind this, no valley of stone to bring the boy home to, no wage. There was only a man who had been alone with what he knew for longer than the boy had been alive, and who had just, without meaning to, stopped.

What he felt was fear. He identified it with some interest, the way he identified rare reagents. Not fear for himself; that reagent he knew. Fear of the arithmetic by which his own likely ending had just acquired a second name in the ledger.

The chit came at midday.

Not a summons. The Chorus summoned by seal, grey paper, wax pressed with the cipher of the Song, a robed clerk who waited while you rose and straightened your coat, the whole liturgy of being sent for. This was a quartermaster's strip, unsealed, and the runner who left it on the corner of the long table was a boy he did not know, and the boy did not wait. By the time Aurelius reached the table the door was swinging shut, and Aldric, at the far stands, had noticed nothing at all.

Five words, in a small, even, characterless hand. The hand of someone who had practiced, for years, the removal of themselves from their own writing.

The star has two names.

He stood with it for a long time.

He turned it over. Blank. He turned it back, and the words had not changed, and he made himself read them as an instrument would read them, for content, for source, for load. It was not a threat. A threat would have named the chamber, or the boy, or the word the boy had let escape. Threats had geometries, and geometries could be mapped; he had been mapping them his whole life. This had no geometry. This was a hand reaching out of the tower's stone to touch him once, precisely, on the exact nerve where his secret was kept, and then withdrawing.

Three souls had been in that chamber. A woman who no longer knew that stars had names at all. A boy who would go to the fire before repeating what he had heard. Himself. And the instruments, which recorded everything and did not record speech, and he had verified that fact personally, years ago, in the years when verifying such facts had seemed like caution rather than confession.

Someone had been in the room who was not in the room. Or someone had not needed to be. Someone who knew what he was well enough to know what he would say to a bird in the dark, and how a moor woman's correction would lodge in him like a seed in a wall, and that of all the sentences in the Empire, these five words were the ones he could neither report nor ignore nor forget.

Under the alarm, threaded through it like the wire through his fingers, ran the older thing. The thing that had made him unbearable as a child and useful as a man and was, he suspected, the only part of him that had never once lied. He was interested. Somewhere in this tower of written names, someone else kept the whispered ones. Someone else knew that the Lamp was the Glede. And they had spent their five words not on threatening him, but on telling him so.

He folded the chit small, the way she had folded a lifetime of fires into one sentence about a boy who did not know the order of the lighting, and he put it in the pocket where the wire lived, and stood at the north window with his hands behind him. Beyond the glass the mountains went up into a sky that had been coming in wrong for some years now, the light souring a little at the edges of the seasons, a grey that the almanacs did not mention and that his eyes, which counted everything, had never been able to stop counting.

Behind him, glass found its place on the rack with a small true sound. The seventh stand, clean.

The evening docket came up with the lamps, as it always did, on grey paper, sealed, correct. Session at the eighth hour tomorrow. Subject: male, sixty years, the Ninth Province, unlicensed observance. He read it standing, and found that the reading had changed and would not change back. Where the ledger said the Ninth Province, something older in him now asked, quietly, and would go on asking:

What does his village call it. What do they call him. What is the second name.
