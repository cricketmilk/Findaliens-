Part One — The Hollowing
Interlude I — The Wrong Sky

There is one impression he has never needed the stone to replay. He sealed it anyway, years ago, onto the private crystal that no drawer holds and no registrar has serviced, because he wanted one record of it that was not made of himself. It plays on its own, some nights. It chose its own hour then too.

It goes like this.

There is grass, and the grass is cold, and the cold is wrong. That is the first fact of this world and he receives it before he receives anything else, before sky, before his own name, of which he has none. The grass is wet in a way that clings. The air moves without meaning anything by it. Everything here happens and does not signify, event without pattern, noise without figure, and the smallness of that, the poverty of it, is the first thing this world ever does to him, and he is a child lying in a dark field, and he has not yet opened his eyes.

He opens them on the wrong stars.

He knows immediately. It is not a thing he works out. Where he comes from, and already the phrase is failing, because where is one of this world's words and does not fit, pattern was not looked at, pattern was lived in, the way the unborn live inside a heartbeat, hearing nothing because it is everything. Here the sky is a scatter. Points thrown on the dark, gapped, crooked, and burning in silence, and something in him reaches for the figure in them, the way a hand reaches in the dark for a door that has always been there, and there is no figure, and there is no door, and the reaching does not stop. He will be an old man and the reaching will not have stopped.

He is found in the morning by a shepherd whose dogs will not come up the fell.

Something else found him first. The stone keeps it before the shepherd, before the word omen: a shadow crossing him at first light, low, and then a young hawk dropping onto the frost beside a child the dogs would not approach. She folded her wings and considered him with one amber eye. She wore no ring and answered no lure. When the shepherd carried the boy down, she rode the air above them, and was on the byre roof in the morning, and the next roof after that.

The villages read her as the omen's familiar and feared him the worse for it. He read her as pattern. She should not have stayed. She should not have lived that long. She violated the arithmetic of the moor, and she did it for no visible gain. It was the first thing in this world that did not add up, and he did not try to fix it. He simply added her to the count.

What follows has the shape of charity and the temperature of fear. The village is seven houses on a moor road, and it passes him along like a debt. He is fed at thresholds. He is prayed over in two faiths, the licensed one and the older one, hedging. Women make a sign near him with the left hand, low, where the chapter house clerk will not see it, and he learns his first word of this world's speech, and it is not a name for him, it is the word for what he is instead of a name, and the word is omen.

He learns their speech in a season. It is easy. Everything here is easy, that is the horror of it, the whole world is four notes played slowly, and he can hear the next one coming before it comes, always, in speech, in weather, in the swing of a gate, in the way the shepherd's wife sets bread down harder on the table the day before she weeps, and he says so, and that is the end of being fed at that threshold. He is seven, or the size the moor calls seven. He has understood the following things, and can prove them, and will tell anyone, because no one has yet taught him that proof is not wanted: that the harvest song does not bring the harvest. That the sign against him does not do anything. That the boy who drowned at the ford was let drown by four men's choices, each one small and sensible, and that the grief in the seven houses runs precisely along the lines of who owed whom, and no other lines. That everything, everything, is strings, and the strings are visible, and nothing is behind them.

They are not wrong to fear him. He would say this now, forty years on, in the flat tone he uses for findings. A child who sees the strings and has not yet found one single thing in the world worth being kind about is no innocent. He is an audit, passing through the houses that feed him. In his eighth autumn, at Hesk's table, the fourth house, with the ford boy three days drowned and the mother sitting dry eyed among the loaned dishes, he said, into the room: "Four men let him drown. I can name them." Hesk's door was the first that shut against him for good.

The winters were the bearable part. This is the strange thing, the thing the impression keeps at its center like a stone in a fruit. Winter on the high moor: lightless by the fourth hour, cold past complaining about, the whole world gone simple. On the worst nights the older faith walked up the fells with fire, he never knew why, was never told, was not the kind of child anyone explained things to, and from whatever doorway currently suffered him he would watch the lights go up the dark one by one, small, stubborn, spaced like a sentence being said slowly, and the reaching thing in him, the thing that groped all day at a sky with no figure in it, would go quiet for as long as the fires burned. He did not know what they were for. He knew what they were like. They were like someone, in all this noise, keeping count.

He is nine, or the size the moor calls nine, the night he does the thing that this impression exists to keep.

He is in the byre loft of the sixth house, not sleeping, sleep having never been a country that would have him, and through the loft's one gap he can see the west, low, over the fold of the fell, one bright star sitting in the black like a lamp in a window. And out of spite, which is the only tool he has, being nine, being a debt passed along a moor road in a world of visible strings, he begins to name the sky. Privately. Point by point. His own catalogue, his own figures, drawn by his own eye, owed to no one. If the sky here has no pattern, he will impose one, that is what the anger is for, and he goes at it corner to corner, leaving nothing unnumbered, and when he comes to the bright one in the west he stops.

Because he has been watching it for four winters, and it is always there, low, at the fold of the fell, at the bottom of the dark. And what he wants, at nine, in the loft, in the wrong world, is so far under his anger that the anger has never once managed to reach down and strangle it, and it comes up now through the gap in the boards with his breath showing and it names the star for him, before he can stop it, with the first word of this world he ever wanted.

The Latch, he calls it. As in: the door is not gone. As in: it is only shut.

He is sixty floors of black stone away from that loft now, and the same star stands low in the west of the same wrong sky, and a moor woman with wax in the creases of her hands has lately told him, in a cold chamber, gently, the way you correct a child's grip on a spoon, that its name is Glede. A banked coal. A fire that looks spent, and is carried through the dark to light the next one.

Three names now, for one star, in one man's keeping.

He has never told anyone the third.
