Part One — The Hollowing
Interlude II — The Early Net

The second impression he sealed in two sittings, years apart, and the seam between them shows. He has never smoothed it. The seam is part of the record.

It goes like this.

He is eleven, and the moor is done with him, and the feeling is mutual in the way of a debt finally called. The chapter house at the market town takes him for a copyist because he can hold figures, all of them, at once, without visible effort, and the clerk who tests him goes quiet in the middle of the test and does not finish it, and he is given a stool and an inkstone and a corner out of the light, which suits every party.

The chapter house has a basin room. The town built it by subscription a generation back, the way the towns built all of them, in the years when the criers still called the web the common weave, and the schools taught, without irony, that the Empire was raising itself one great shared mind, wisdom held in common the way water is held in common, a well in every village and every child free to drink. The subscription plaque is still bolted by the door, forty names, masons and carters and one midwife, the people who paid for the well. The licensed men who now decide who draws from it are newer than the plaque, and walk past it daily, and have never once read it.

He is not supposed to enter it. It is not forbidden; forbidding it would require imagining that anyone wanted in; it is simply a room for licensed men, where the paired basins stand on their stone tables and tremble. Two vessels of one water, distilled together, rippling together forever after, no matter the distance. The clerks read the ripples the way farmers read cloud: slowly, by rote, wrong as often as right. He stands in the doorway one evening with a message nobody needs, and he sees the surface of the nearest basin move, and the world, which has been four notes played slowly since the night he fell into it, opens one more door.

Because the ripple is not weather. The ripple is figure. It is the first patterned thing he has met on this side of the sky, and he walks toward it as the reaching in him has been trying to walk for six years, and the basin room becomes, over the seasons that follow, the place he lives, in every hour the licensed men do not want it, which is most hours. The clerks think the omen boy has found religion. Let them. What he has found is the net.

The basins talk to basins. The ledger webs ride the same resonance, tallies moving between counting houses, grain numbers, tax numbers, the Empire's pulse taken hourly by men who only ever read their own wrist of it. And nobody, so far as he can tell, nobody anywhere, reads the whole. The traffic itself, the shape of all of it moving, lies open like a night sky, and this sky has figures. He is twelve years old and he can see the Empire breathe. He watches harvest move through it like weather fronts. He watches rumor outrun riders. He learns the pulse of the Fifth Province's banks, the tidal drag of Vessen grain, the way a border scare shows in the salt price two days before it shows in any dispatch, and he does what he did in the byre loft with the stars, privately, point by point, owed to no one: he makes the catalogue.

The first composing arrays reach the market towns the year he turns thirteen. Machines that write. Hymn settings assembled to order, festival verses, letters of condolence for the bereaved of two faiths, and the clerks feed them occasions and read out what returns and marvel, and he stands in his corner and is the only person in the room who is afraid. Not of the verses. The verses are bad. He is afraid because he can see what the array is doing, which is nothing, gloriously nothing, pattern without a patterner, the sound of a mind with no one home in it, and it is the first thing this world has ever shown him that resembles the far side of the sky, and it has been put to work writing condolences. He goes home the long way that night, and in the sealed impression the thought is still there, a boy's thought in a boy's grammar, intact after a third of a century like the button in the registrar's tray: they have built a little door to the place I fell from, and they are using it to lie to widows.

That is the first discovery. The second takes longer, because the second requires believing that everyone else is not doing what he is doing, which takes him years to credit, and the second seam of the impression begins here.

He keeps his own readings. Of everything; that is not new; but the sky above all, because the sky is where he fell from and he has never once stopped auditing it. Cloud, light, the browning of certain sunsets, the lateness of certain rains, forty little series in a private notation, kept the way other boys keep birds' eggs. And the series drift. Slowly, patiently, all in one direction, a souring at the edges of the seasons, small enough each year to be weather, consistent enough across years to be nothing of the kind. Meanwhile the almanacs arrive from the printing houses each autumn, correct to the hour on every eclipse and tide, and on the drift, silent. Not wrong. Silent. He checks their tables against his own for three years before he will allow himself the finding, because the finding is enormous and he is fourteen and he has learned by then what happens when he tells people what he can prove.

The finding is this: the numbers know. The narrative does not. And the distance between what the Empire's instruments say and what the Empire is told is not ignorance, because ignorance drifts and this holds steady, and what holds steady is held. Someone is keeping the two apart, with care, at scale, the way a clerk keeps two ledgers.

He is fourteen, and he has discovered, in a basin room in a market town, with an inkstone and a private notation, that the world is run on a discrepancy.

He tells no one. This is the part of the impression he replays least and sealed most exactly. It is not fear, or not only. It is arithmetic. He has run the transaction a hundred times: the omen boy, the moor accent, the eyes that track wrong, walking into the prefect's hall with a boy's notebook to report that the sky is being lied about, and every path through the sum ends the same place, ends in the left hand making its low sign, and nothing changing, except for him. So he closes the notebook. He is precisely, coldly, provably right, and he closes the notebook, and the closing makes a sound he has been hearing ever since.

And in the traffic, that same year, he watches the quiet begin.

He has names for the flows by then, his own names, whispered ones. There is a kind of traffic he calls crossing water, the letters and levies and small stubborn motions that gather around certain men in the provincial assemblies, the ones who ask what the tithes buy, the ones whose correspondence smells of many small hands rather than a few large ones. He likes watching crossing water. It moves against the grain and survives. And in his fifteenth year it begins, node by node, to go still. A tribune reassigned upcountry, and his traffic stops. A jurist's name gone from the levy lists, and his traffic stops. One by one, over two years, in an order he cannot fault, an order too clean to be chance, the against the grain motions of the Empire resolve into the grain, and the net grows quieter, and the quiet is not peace, the quiet is tuning, and the boy in the corner of the basin room watches the silencing happen in the only place it was ever visible, which is the place nobody else was looking.

When the charter is proclaimed the following spring, a gift of the Emperor, a stone for every subject at fifteen, the clerks in the chapter house weep with the beauty of it, and he stands among them doing the one thing he does, and there is no surprise anywhere in him. He had watched the ground being cleared. He knew what the mandate would be before it had a name, the way you know the next note of a four note song.

He is fifteen, which is charter age. The recruiters come through the market towns that summer with lists.

The impression ends there, on the seam. What is on the far side of the seam belongs to another stone.

He replays this one now, at a north window, at dawn, a hundred pages of provisioning locked behind him on a shelf he can find in the dark, and the boy's two discoveries stand in the lamplight of three decades and more and are not boys' discoveries anymore. They have annexes now. They have a date. The little door they built to the place he fell from has been put to work writing endings, and the discrepancy the world is run on has a schedule, and somewhere under all of it, unpaid, accruing, in a notation no one else has ever read, is the question he closed the notebook on at fourteen.

He had been right. He had told no one.

Both of those facts have been waiting for him, all this time, like a drawer that was never closed.
