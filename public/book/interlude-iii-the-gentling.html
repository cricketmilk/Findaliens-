Part One — The Hollowing
Interlude III — The Gentling

The third impression is sealed in batches, across the better part of a decade, and the early batches and the late ones do not sound like the work of the same man. He has never resequenced them. The drift is the record.

It goes like this.

He is thirty, or near it, and he arrives at the surgery in the border town as a patient, because that is what is left. The escape years are behind him by then, and the stone holds them only in summary, by his own choice, a ledger line for a decade: tinctures tried, disciplines tried, deprivations tried, the sight burned at and starved at and never once put out. What the summary does not hold, and what arrives at the surgery on his behalf, is the wreckage. Hands scarred to the wrist by his own experiments. A body he has treated like an apparatus suspected of a fault. Senses rubbed so raw that a market day can put him on the ground.

The physician is a woman named Marrow, and she is older than him, and she has no patience for pieties, which in the border country is a medical specialization of its own. She sets carters' bones and pulls millers' teeth and delivers children at both ends of the valley, wiping her hands on one apron for all three trades, and she examines the strange man with the scarred hands for the better part of an hour in silence, and then she says the first thing the stone keeps of her, which is: "Whoever has been treating you should be drowned. Who was it?"

"Myself," he says.

"Then you see my difficulty," says Marrow.

She does not cure him. She does something no one has ever done, which is worse and better: she takes him seriously. Not the omen, not the strangeness, the findings. He tells her, because she asks in a tone that expects answers, what he was trying to do in the decade the summary covers, and she listens the way he watches, entirely, and at the end she does not make the left hand sign, and does not pray, and does not tell him what he was trying to do was impossible. She says: "You were working on the right problem with the wrong patient. Grief is not a fault in the instrument. Sit down. I have forty of these in this valley and no tools at all."

The partnership is never named as one. There is no charter. There is a back room, and then a second back room, and then the surgery is the clinic and the clinic is the two of them: her hands, his art; her patients, his preparations; her flat unimpressible mercy and his sight, turned for the first time in its existence toward a use. He is a man who has spent ten years learning, on the only subject he had, how a self is fastened together and where it can be made to let go. It has never once occurred to him that the knowledge runs forward as well as back. Marrow makes it occur to him. That is her whole contribution, and it is the largest contribution anyone will ever make to anything he does: she stands in a stone room that smells of carbolic and says, in effect, you have spent a decade drawing a map of hell for your own use. Now turn it over. It is the same map.

The work, in the early batches, is the happiest thing the private stone holds.

They learn to take the weight out of a memory and leave the memory standing. That is the whole art, stated once. The widow Casse, who cannot stop rehearsing her husband's last hour, who has worn a path in her own mind between the bed and the door and walks it forty times a day, sits in the back room with a taper and one of his earliest gentling preparations, and tells the hour aloud while the tincture holds her edges soft, and the telling, for the first time in three years, costs less than everything. And again, the next week, costing less. The memory is not touched. The price of it comes down. The last hour becomes a thing that happened, a true thing, a sad thing, a thing the size of other things, and the widow Casse plants her garden that spring, and brings them eggs, and the stone keeps the eggs, the actual brown eggs in the actual straw, because the man sealing the impression wanted never to forget that this is what the work was for.

The attunements are the other half of the work, and the stone keeps them the way it keeps the eggs. The art arrives sideways, in the third year, the way his true findings always arrive: a preparation meant to soften one soul's edges softens, instead, the seam between two, and the two, if they consent, if they reach, knit. Attunement, Marrow names it, because she names things the way she splints, plainly, for use, and what they can join, in those years, is anything that will hold still for love. A deaf weaver's daughter to her mother's loom, so the shuttle's rhythm lives in the girl's bones and the house's trade is hers despite her ears. A blind ferryman to his river, the water's grain arriving in him like weather, so that he dies, decades on, the oldest working boatman on that crossing. An old shepherd to his older dog, because the two of them were failing alone and refused to do it apart. Two sisters, one marrying across the valley, joined so that the distance never becomes what distance was for everyone else. And once, the two of them drunk on the art at the end of a hard winter, a whole grieving household attuned to its own hearth, so the house stops being the box a death happened in and goes back to being where a family lives.

The bonds do not break. That is the finding that will follow him the length of his life, in both directions: made in consent and kept in kindness, an attunement outlasts everything the border years test it against, distance, fever, quarrel, and, as he will learn far too late and far too well, death itself. Nothing they join in that surgery ever comes undone. People walk out of the back room larger than they walked in, seamed to their trades and their rivers and each other, and the oldest illness in the world, the loneliness of the one body, is treated, for the first time in the history of physic, at a chandler's bench in a border town, for eggs. He will spend the rest of his life watching what the age does with the art: the bond forced onto wings for war and dosed to a schedule until it wears its people to the graying; the joining rationed, licensed, collared; and the word itself stolen, last and worst, hung by the Chorus on its emptied so that attuned came to mean the precise opposite of joined. None of it ever reaches the border years. They stay in the stone the way the eggs stay: the art as it was, before anyone priced it. They had found the way to make two things one, and for six years the only use anyone put it to was love.

The sounding is the deepest room of the work, and the stone keeps his own.

They build it for the cases the taper and the telling cannot reach, the hurts seated below the floor a voice can stand on, and it is not a preparation. It is a passage. The sounding, Marrow calls it, a boatman's word, for what you do to find the bottom of dark water. Behind the second back room they dig a third, dry stone and dark, a room with no weather and no hour in it; wool underfoot, lamp oil, the small bell she rings at the turnings; water at blood heat in a slate cistern, so the body loses its edges and stops reporting. The gentling opens the grain, taken at the threshold, in the dark, at the pace of the count. Then the guided part, which is the whole of it. Deprivation first, until the senses let go of the world they have been gripping; then one thing at a time, chosen, meant: a smell of beebalm at the right turning, a name said aloud, a hand at the sternum, cold iron to close. And somewhere past the bottom of the dark the senses give up their borders and pour together, hearing arriving as weight, the bell tasting of slate, the body experienced at last from the inside as one continuous instrument, and in that fusion the self can be reached at depths no surface art has ever touched. The nerves learn what is worked there without knowing they are learning.

And because what is opened that deep must be closed that deep, there is the second vial, which is not a vial but a course. The mordant, he names it in the casebook, out of the dyers' sheds: the reagent that fixes the color in the cloth, so that what the vat changed the washing cannot take back. Reagents and simples and the humblest kitchen supplements, iron and salts and bitter greens, dosed through the days of the treatment, and it does no work of its own. It only keeps the work. A soul dyed new in the deep room and left unfixed fades by seasons back to its old color; the same soul, held in the mordant while the pattern sets, keeps it, and the stone holds the finding in his own dry hand: change is cheap. Keeping is the art.

He is the first soul the full passage is ever worked on, at his own insistence, over Marrow's objection, which she registers once and then abandons, because she has met him. He designs his own descent, every stimulus chosen, every turning scored like a page of music, and goes down into his own dark with her voice for a floor, and the stone keeps what he finds at the bottom, and it is not the pattern country, and it is not the door. It is the heartbeat. The cold night, the grass, the terror, the reaching, and under her voice, at depth, with his senses running together like waters, the reaching finally closes on something, because the something was never on the far side at all. It was the body. It had been the body the whole time, the one going concern, the loom he had been woven on twice, and he comes up out of the cistern after a day and a night having signed a peace with the body that carries him, and no year afterward ever fully repeals it. His hands stop being apparatus. The moor's food acquires taste. He sleeps, that winter, the first whole nights of his exile. A decade of deprivations tried alone had nearly killed him, and the difference, he writes in the casebook afterward, was never the dark. It was the voice in it, and the meaning of the things that came, and the fixing after. If the gentling was what the age would steal, the sounding was what it would never even find: proof, worked once on the strangest patient in the world, that a self is not only a thing that can be taken apart. It is a thing that can be finished.

The waiting room never empties. He notices the fact and files it, in those years, under success. It will be fifteen more years before he reads the file correctly: that the valley's supply of the broken was not weather, that the grief and the ruin and the hollow eyed came off a loom somewhere upstream, and that he and Marrow, with their tapers and their tinctures, were the loom's own darning needle, mending what it frayed at a rate it had already priced in.

The Verse comes through the border country in their sixth year.

It comes as pamphlets first, then as criers, then as neighbors. The thousandth verse, the hidden one, the one that names the true enemies of harmony, and the stone's batches from that year hold the clinic's waiting room filling with a new kind of patient: not grieving, not broken. Full. Lit from inside by a story that explains everything, hollowed by it in a way he has no instrument yet to measure, families in the room begging them to give back the fathers and sisters who sit right there, present, warm, and entirely replaced. Marrow, who can set any break she can put a name to, has no tools for this either, and says so in her flat way: "You cannot lance a story."

He tries anyway. That is the batch where the late seam begins. Because the gentling works, after a fashion: the fervor can be softened, the grip loosened, the Verse taker brought down out of the story like a man talked down off a roof. And then the vacancy that is left behind, the socket where the story sat, does not heal shut. It waits. And within a season the Verse is back in it, or a worse one is, because a self emptied of its burning is not restored, it is available, and the stone holds the night he formulates that finding, at Marrow's table, both of them silent a long time afterward.

The vessel, once emptied, fills from the nearest story. He writes it in his casebook as a clinical caution. He believes, writing it, that he is recording a limit of medicine. He is drafting, in a border surgery, by lamplight, in his own hand, the founding doctrine of the age, and the age will come and collect it.

The last batch on the stone is short.

It holds a single evening: the two of them at the bench, the winter of the sixth year, and his newest preparation in its vial, the seventh refinement of the gentling, the one that softens so deep and so clean that the widow Casse's whole path could be unmade in an hour, and neither of them saying, for a long time, the thing that is in the room. Because he has run the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is this: the preparation has become general. It no longer needs his sight, his care, the taper, the telling, the slow paid down mercy of the early work. What he holds in his hand will empty anything, gently, for anyone who administers it, toward any purpose, and the filling afterward will belong to whoever stands nearest with a story, and he says some part of this aloud, finally, badly.

Marrow takes the vial and holds it up against the lamp, the way she held everything, teeth and splinters and newborns, judging soundness by light.

"This will be a mercy," she says.

And the stone ends there, mid evening, because the man who sealed it could not, then or ever, bear to carry the impression one sentence further, to whatever was said next, to whoever knocked at whatever later door. The seam at the end of the third impression is the cleanest cut on the whole private stone.

He replays it now, the night before he rides west, with a closure form in his case bearing the name of a woman from Harrow Fell, and the eggs are still in the straw, and the vial is still in the lamplight, and Marrow is still saying it, in her flat, unimpressible, entirely accurate voice, down all the years:

a mercy. A mercy. A mercy.
