## Historical anchors (established facts you may use)
- On the night of 2-3 December 1984 methyl isocyanate escaped from a plant operated by Union Carbide India Limited (UCIL) at Bhopal. The Second Circuit described it as "the most devastating industrial disaster in history--the deaths of over 2,000 persons and injuries of over 200,000".
- UCIL was an Indian company: 50.9% of its stock was owned by Union Carbide Corporation (UCC), 22% was owned or controlled by the government of India, and the balance was held by roughly 23,500 Indian citizens. It ran 14 plants in India, employed over 9,000 Indian citizens, and was "managed and operated entirely by Indians in India".
- Some 145 purported class actions were filed in US federal courts and assigned to the Southern District of New York. On 29 March 1985 India enacted the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, giving the Union of India the exclusive right to represent the victims "in India or elsewhere".
- Judge Keenan dismissed on forum non conveniens on 12 May 1986 (634 F. Supp. 842) on three conditions. The Second Circuit deleted two of them — the consent to enforcement of a final Indian judgment and the submission to Federal Rules discovery — and affirmed as modified.
- By orders of 14 and 15 February 1989 the Supreme Court of India approved a settlement of "all litigations, claims, rights and liabilities related to and arising out of the [Bhopal] disaster", under which UCC and UCIL agreed to pay $470 million. On 22 December 1989, in Charan Lal Sahu v. Union of India, that Court upheld the constitutional validity of the Bhopal Act and the Indian Government's exclusive authority to compromise all claims.
- In 1993 the Second Circuit held in Bi v. Union Carbide that individual victims lacked standing to challenge the settlement negotiated between Union Carbide and India. Keep the claimants' grounds labelled as contentions, not findings, and do not state a per-victim figure: none was read.
- The India Code page for the 1985 Act could not be opened (edge error), so the Act is described only as the two federal opinions describe it.
