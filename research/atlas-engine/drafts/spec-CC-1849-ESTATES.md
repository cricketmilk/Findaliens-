## Historical anchors (established facts you may use)
- The Great Irish Famine (1845–52) collapsed Irish rents and land values; the potato failures of 1845–49 killed on the order of a million people and drove mass emigration, while landlords’ rental income failed and their encumbrances — mortgages and judgement debts, owed largely to English and London lenders — remained fixed.
- The Encumbered Estates Act 1849 (passed August 1849) established the Encumbered Estates Court with power to sell encumbered Irish estates quickly, overriding prior judicial and settlement constraints on sale, and to pay the proceeds to creditors ahead of other interests.
- The court sat from 1849–50; sales began in 1850, and estates fetched famine-depression prices far below pre-famine values. On the order of three thousand estates had been sold by the time the court was superseded — a widely cited figure in the scholarship.
- The Landed Estates Court (Ireland) Act 1858 (21 & 22 Vict. c. 72) replaced the Encumbered Estates Court with the Landed Estates Court, retaining and extending the same liquidation machinery; further acts in 1861 and 1866 amended it, and the court’s jurisdiction passed to the High Court of Justice in Ireland in the late 1870s.
- The transfer changed the owners, not the terms of tenure: tenant farmers gained no security from the sales, eviction-driven clearance continued, and the creditors’ liquidation that sold the land while leaving tenancy untouched is documented in scholarship as presaging the land-question politics of the 1870s–80s (Land League, 1879 onward).
- The 1858 parliamentary record preserves the transfer debate: an 1858 Commons supply debate records commissioners of the Encumbered Estates Court and a Land Transfer Office being accommodated in new law buildings, and the same session’s Commons committee debate on the Sale and Transfer of Land (Ireland) Bill records the parliamentary-title principle argued for encumbered estates sold under the machinery.
- A parliamentary notice of the 1858 transfer states that the Transfer of Land Bill extended the powers hitherto exercised by the Incumbered Estates Commissioners and facilitated an indefeasible title for purchasers of land — contemporary record that the 1858 act carried the 1849 court’s liquidation machinery forward under its period spelling.
- In the 11 May 1858 Lords third-reading debate on the bill, Lord Cranworth framed the measure as following the Royal Commission’s report on the Irish encumbered estates courts, and Lord St Leonards stated on the record that Sir Robert Peel had authored the original Incumbered Estates Act — speakers’ claims preserved in Hansard, not census figures.
