## Historical anchors (established facts you may use)
- The panic of May 1893 forced receiverships on a wave of American railroads; contemporary counts put more than 70 roads in receivership by 1895, among them Atchison Topeka & Santa Fe, Union Pacific, Northern Pacific, Philadelphia & Reading and the Erie.
- Receivers were appointed by courts of equity, typically on application of creditors or friendly interests; the same financial houses that had underwritten the roads' securities supplied the receivers and dominated the reorganization committees.
- The receivership kept the road operating and routed income to fixed charges, while reorganization committees marshalled junior security holders into depositing securities under threat of exclusion from the reorganized company — a practice contemporaries called "friendly receivership".
- Reorganizations closed via foreclosure sales conveying the properties to new companies organized by the same interests; the wave drew sustained criticism and helped drive later railroad reorganization reform (culminating in Section 77, 1933).
- The citations for this dossier are CourtListener records of a later (1912) Washington Supreme Court receivership opinion; the 1893-95 wave is the historical context, not the litigated case.
