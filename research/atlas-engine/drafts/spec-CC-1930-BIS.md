## Historical anchors (established facts you may use)
- The Bank for International Settlements: created by the Hague Agreements of January 1930 to administer Germany's WWI reparations under the Young Plan (which had restructured the 1924 Dawes Plan schedules), owned jointly by major central banks, seated in Basel beyond any single nation's jurisdiction, with legal immunity for its assets and archives written into its constituent charters.
- The Depression destroyed the reparations flow it was built to manage: Hoover Moratorium (1931), then Lausanne (1932) effectively ended German payments — but the institution did not dissolve; it repurposed.
- During WWII it stayed open in neutral Switzerland, continued gold and foreign-exchange operations with all belligerents, and its American president Thomas H. McKittrick kept channels to both Allied and Axis central banks. In March 1939, after the German occupation of Prague, the BIS accepted the transfer of Czechoslovak National Bank gold to the Reichsbank — the "Czech gold" affair that scandalised Westminster.
- At Bretton Woods (July 1944) the conference passed a resolution calling for the BIS's liquidation "at the earliest possible moment"; the dissolution never happened — the 1948 post-war settlement left it standing as the central bankers' club.
- Post-war it became the seat of the Basel Committee and the quiet architect of global bank capital standards — the unelected machine for sovereign and bank debt machinery, born from a debt trap and immune by design.
